# UniPathway: Business Management and Health/Social Care Lesson Batch

Eight original teaching lessons for an indicative Level 2–5 progression. Levels describe increasing complexity within this batch, not accreditation, credit value or verified alignment to an awarding body's qualification. A qualified curriculum lead must map and review these lessons before formal programme use. Suggested duration: 60–75 minutes each.

Care lessons use fictional adult community-support settings only. They teach nonclinical communication, service organisation and improvement, not diagnosis, treatment, medication, personal-care procedures or legal advice. Learners should follow their organisation's approved policies and seek supervision for real situations. Never use identifiable service-user information in class.

## UP-BM-L2-01: Business Management Level 2 | Planning a reliable workday

### Objectives
By the end, learners can distinguish urgent from important work; sequence tasks with dependencies; and produce a practical daily plan with a contingency allowance.

### Explanation and walkthrough
Urgency describes how soon an action is needed. Importance describes its contribution to an agreed outcome. A loud request is not automatically the highest priority. A dependency is something that must happen before another activity can start.

Fictional community shop Northbridge has one administrator working 09:00–12:00, or 180 minutes. Today's work is: check a delivery list (20 minutes), prepare customer collection labels (30 minutes, after checking the list), resolve a supplier quantity query (25 minutes, before the supplier's 10:00 deadline), update a display plan (40 minutes) and answer routine messages (35 minutes). Total planned work is 150 minutes, leaving 30 minutes for interruptions.

A defensible sequence is 09:00 supplier query; 09:25 delivery check; 09:45 labels; 10:15 messages; 10:50 display plan; 11:30 contingency. The supplier deadline is protected, labels use verified information, and the plan does not pretend every minute is predictable. Record the responsible person and completion evidence, such as an acknowledged supplier response, rather than only writing “done”.

### Practical task
The delivery list will not arrive until 09:40. Replan the morning without missing the supplier deadline or preparing labels from unverified data. State two assumptions and one escalation point.

### Indicative answer
09:00–09:25 supplier query; 09:25–09:40 routine messages; 09:40–10:00 list check; 10:00–10:30 labels; 10:30–10:50 remaining messages; 10:50–11:30 display plan; 11:30–12:00 contingency. Assumptions: tasks can be interrupted between messages and the list arrives complete at 09:40. Escalate to the supervisor if the list is still missing at 10:00, explaining which collections may be delayed. Other sequences earn credit if dependencies, deadlines and realistic capacity are preserved.

### Facilitation and assessment
Use task cards in pairs, then introduce the late-list change. Ask why each priority was chosen. Check calculations and dependency reasoning, not decorative presentation. Support learners with a blank timetable; extend by adding an urgent task and requiring a justified trade-off.

## UP-BM-L3-01: Business Management Level 3 | Contribution and break-even decisions

### Objectives
Calculate unit contribution and break-even volume; distinguish revenue from contribution and profit; and explain the limits of a simple break-even model.

### Explanation and walkthrough
Revenue is selling price multiplied by sales volume. Variable costs change with units supplied; fixed costs remain unchanged within the period and capacity range assumed. Contribution per unit is price less variable cost. Contribution first covers fixed costs; only the remainder is operating profit in this simplified model.

Northbridge sells workshop places at £30 each. Materials and facilitator costs attributable to each place are £12. Monthly room and administration costs are £900. Contribution is £18 per place. Break-even is £900 ÷ £18 = 50 places. At 60 places, revenue is £1,800, variable costs £720 and contribution £1,080, leaving £180 after fixed costs. This is not a cash-flow forecast: payment timing, tax and investment spending are excluded.

### Practical task
A proposed discount reduces price to £27 while variable cost stays £12. Calculate the new contribution, minimum whole places needed to break even, and simplified profit at 60 places. Would you approve the discount solely on these figures?

### Indicative answer
Contribution is £15. Break-even is 60 places. At 60 places, revenue £1,620 minus variable costs £720 minus fixed costs £900 gives £0. The previous £180 surplus disappears. Do not approve solely from these figures: investigate expected additional demand, capacity, customer access, competitors and whether costs really remain constant. At 61 places the model gives a £15 surplus, but that alone does not establish commercial success.

### Facilitation and assessment
Model one calculation, then have pairs explain each other's working. Award credit separately for arithmetic and interpretation. Extension: at capacity of 55 places, learners explain why the discounted offer cannot break even under the stated assumptions. Avoid presenting the exercise as advice about a real business's tax or finances.

## UP-BM-L4-01: Business Management Level 4 | Diagnosing a service-process bottleneck

### Objectives
Map a process, compare stage capacities using consistent units, and propose a measurable improvement without sacrificing quality.

### Explanation and walkthrough
A process transforms an input into an output through linked stages. The bottleneck is the stage limiting sustained throughput under the stated conditions. Faster work elsewhere may create queues rather than increase completed output. Capacity is not guaranteed demand or actual output; errors, breaks and variation can reduce it.

A fictional training provider handles registration packs through three sequential stages, each with one dedicated worker: completeness check, 6 minutes per pack; eligibility review, 10 minutes; confirmation, 5 minutes. Each worker has 300 productive minutes daily. Capacities are 50, 30 and 60 packs daily respectively. With adequate demand, the eligibility stage limits the whole process to 30 packs daily. Buying faster confirmation software alone does not remove that constraint.

An improvement might use a clearer applicant checklist to reduce missing information. Test whether it reduces reviewer rework; do not simply remove eligibility checks to obtain a faster headline result.

### Practical task
A pilot reduces average eligibility-review time to 7.5 minutes. Calculate revised stage and process capacities. Design a one-week comparison with one speed measure and two quality or fairness safeguards.

### Indicative answer
Eligibility capacity becomes 300 ÷ 7.5 = 40 packs; other stages remain 50 and 60. Process capacity is now 40 packs daily under the assumptions. Measure median elapsed time from complete pack to confirmed decision, alongside incorrect-decision/rework rate and differences in completion or rejection patterns between relevant applicant groups using appropriately governed data. Compare similar volumes and case complexity, record staffing changes and report sample size. One week provides preliminary evidence, not proof of sustained benefit. A result that improves speed while increasing erroneous rejection is not a successful improvement.

### Facilitation and assessment
Draw a three-stage flow and use counters to simulate queues. Ask learners to distinguish processing time from waiting time. Assess calculations, a feasible comparison and explicit safeguards. Extension: introduce a mix of simple and complex applications and ask why a single average may mislead.

## UP-BM-L5-01: Business Management Level 5 | Choosing a strategy under uncertainty

### Objectives
Construct a transparent option appraisal, test sensitivity to assumptions, and recommend a staged decision with review conditions.

### Explanation and walkthrough
Strategic choices combine evidence, priorities and uncertainty. A weighted score is a decision aid, not an objective truth. Define criteria before scoring, explain the scale, document evidence and identify non-negotiable requirements separately. A high total must never override an essential safety or feasibility constraint.

A fictional learning service considers A: expanding evening provision and B: opening a second centre. Scores use 1 = weak and 5 = strong. For access (weight 0.40), A scores 4 and B 5; financial resilience (0.35), A 4 and B 2; delivery feasibility (0.25), A 5 and B 2. A totals 1.60 + 1.40 + 1.25 = 4.25. B totals 2.00 + 0.70 + 0.50 = 3.20. These scores are illustrative judgments, not researched claims. A seems preferable, but its staffing assumptions need validation.

### Practical task
Recalculate with access weight 0.60, resilience 0.25 and feasibility 0.15. Write a recommendation including a pilot, two evidence needs and a stop/review condition. Explain why neither score authorises spending.

### Indicative answer
A = 2.40 + 1.00 + 0.75 = 4.15. B = 3.00 + 0.50 + 0.30 = 3.80. A remains ahead, but the gap narrows from 1.05 to 0.35. Recommend a limited evening pilot subject to approval, demand evidence and verified staffing capacity. Measure attendance, completion and full delivery costs against agreed targets. Pause expansion if staffing cannot support the promised service or the agreed budget limit is breached. Decision-makers still need reliable costs, stakeholder consultation, authorisation and any applicable professional review. Changing weights cannot manufacture missing evidence.

### Facilitation and assessment
Assign groups different stakeholder priorities, then compare recommendations. Assess disclosure of assumptions and willingness to revise, not agreement with A. Extension: ask learners what new evidence could reverse their conclusion and how to avoid optimistic scoring of a favoured option.

## UP-HSC-L2-01: Health and Social Care Level 2 | Respectful communication and choice

### Objectives
Use open questions and plain language; distinguish a person's stated preference from an assumption; and check understanding without pressuring agreement.

### Explanation and walkthrough
Person-centred communication starts with the individual, not a stereotype. Introduce your role, explain the available options accurately, ask what matters to the person and allow time to respond. An open question invites explanation; a closed question confirms a specific detail. Checking understanding is about how clearly you explained something, not testing intelligence.

In a fictional adult community activity centre, Sam asks about joining an afternoon group. A worker says: “There are art and conversation groups today. Would you like to hear about either, or would you prefer something else?” Sam says the room is noisy. Instead of assuming Sam dislikes people, the worker asks what would make attendance more comfortable and explains only genuinely available options. A check might be: “To make sure I explained it clearly, what would you like to happen next?” Sam may decline; participation is not compulsory in this scenario.

### Practical task
Rewrite: “You will enjoy the group. Everyone your age does. I'll put you down for it.” Include an open question, a real choice and an understanding check. Identify what should be recorded.

### Indicative answer
“Would you like me to explain today's activities? What interests you, and is there anything that would make joining easier? You can choose not to join. Have I explained the options clearly, and what would you prefer next?” Record Sam's expressed preference and any agreed practical arrangement using the centre's approved process, not labels such as “uncooperative” or assumptions about a condition. Do not promise a quiet room unless one is available.

### Facilitation and assessment
Use short paired role-play with an observer checklist. Learners may observe instead of performing. Do not require personal disclosures or impersonations of disabilities. Assess listening, choice, accurate explanations and respectful wording. Discuss when to ask a supervisor for communication support rather than guessing.

## UP-HSC-L3-01: Health and Social Care Level 3 | Accurate nonclinical records and handovers

### Objectives
Separate observation, reported information and interpretation; produce a concise factual handover; and identify when uncertainty needs supervisor clarification.

### Explanation and walkthrough
A useful service record states what happened, when, who provided information and what action was agreed. Observation is something directly witnessed. Reported information identifies its source. Interpretation is an explanation that may be wrong; avoid presenting it as fact. Record only relevant information through approved systems and share it only through authorised channels.

At 14:10 in a fictional community support centre, Lee arrives for a booked transport pickup and says, “The driver told me it would be 14:30.” The schedule shows 14:00. The worker checks with the transport coordinator at 14:15 and receives a revised pickup time of 14:35, which Lee accepts. An appropriate note records those times, the discrepancy and the agreed update. “Lee was confused again and the driver was careless” adds unsupported judgments and omits the useful action.

### Practical task
Write a handover of no more than 90 words. State one uncertainty and the next responsible role. Explain why posting it in a public staff social-media group is inappropriate.

### Indicative answer
“At 14:10 Lee was waiting for transport booked on the schedule for 14:00. Lee reported being told 14:30 by the driver. At 14:15 the transport coordinator confirmed a revised pickup time of 14:35. I informed Lee, who accepted this arrangement. The original cause of the time discrepancy is unverified. The next shift coordinator should check whether pickup occurred and follow the centre's approved escalation process if it did not.” Public posting exposes personal service information outside an authorised handover process; use the approved restricted system instead.

### Facilitation and assessment
Colour-code fact, reported information and interpretation in sample notes. Assess completeness, relevance and neutrality. Do not reward unnecessary detail. This scenario contains no clinical assessment or emergency; real concerns must be escalated through local approved procedures rather than resolved from this exercise.

## UP-HSC-L4-01: Health and Social Care Level 4 | Improving access without creating new barriers

### Objectives
Analyse a service-access problem; design a small improvement test with service-user involvement; and evaluate a balancing measure alongside the main outcome.

### Explanation and walkthrough
An improvement should make the service better for the people using it, not just easier to administer. A small test defines the change, who is affected, how success will be measured and what could worsen. A balancing measure checks for that unintended harm.

A fictional adult community centre has 40 booked activity visits each week. In a baseline week, eight visits are missed: 8 ÷ 40 = 20%. Staff propose a voluntary reminder method chosen by each participant. They retain non-digital booking and do not send messages until the participant's preference and the organisation's approved contact process are confirmed. In a pilot week, four of 40 visits are missed: 10%. That is a 10-percentage-point reduction, or a 50% relative reduction. It does not prove reminders caused the change: weather, transport and participant mix may differ.

### Practical task
Design a four-week pilot using fictional data only. Specify the main measure, two balancing measures, a way to hear from people who dislike digital contact, and a pause condition.

### Indicative answer
Track missed visits divided by booked visits each week, with counts as well as percentages. Track unwanted-contact reports and staff time spent arranging reminders. Invite feedback by telephone or in person as well as digitally, without making feedback a condition of support. Keep an equally usable non-digital option. Record major external changes such as transport disruption. Pause and review a contact method if it reaches the wrong recipient or repeated unwanted-contact reports arise; follow the organisation's incident procedures rather than improvising disclosure. Four weeks offers learning, not a universal effectiveness claim.

### Facilitation and assessment
Ask groups to challenge a “digital-only” proposal from different perspectives without stereotyping. Assess denominators, accessible feedback and credible safeguards. Extend by changing weekly attendance totals so learners must avoid comparing raw missed-visit counts alone.

## UP-HSC-L5-01: Health and Social Care Level 5 | Governance of a community-support change

### Objectives
Distinguish accountability from task allocation; compare benefits, risks and evidence gaps; and design a proportionate decision and review structure for a nonclinical change.

### Explanation and walkthrough
Governance clarifies who may decide, who performs the work, who reviews evidence and how concerns are escalated. A manager delegating tasks does not make accountability disappear. Service-user involvement should influence decisions rather than simply endorse a finished plan. A risk register is useful only if each significant risk has an owner, a practical control and a review point.

A fictional adult community-support service proposes centralising activity bookings across two centres. Potential benefits are fewer duplicate bookings and a single contact point. Risks include reduced local knowledge, inaccessible contact methods and inappropriate record access. The service lead owns the decision; a project coordinator manages implementation; authorised information-governance and accessibility leads review their respective areas; participants and frontline staff help design and test the process. These are illustrative project responsibilities, not a prescription for regulated staffing or legal compliance.

### Practical task
Prepare a decision brief with: one intended outcome; three risks with owners and controls; an approval gate; a pilot review; and a rollback condition. Include a concern-reporting route that does not depend solely on the project's lead supporter.

### Indicative answer
Outcome: reduce duplicate bookings without making access harder. Risks: (1) inaccessible central contact, owned by the service lead, controlled by preserving local/in-person booking and testing with participants; (2) unnecessary record access, owned by the authorised information-governance lead, controlled by approved role-based access review before rollout; (3) lost bookings during transition, owned by the project coordinator, controlled by reconciled fictional test records and an approved recovery plan. Approval gate: accountable service leadership reviews evidence and required internal approvals before any real migration. Review a limited pilot for booking accuracy, accessibility feedback and complaints, not speed alone. Pause and use the approved fallback if bookings cannot be reconciled or a material access problem is unresolved. Provide the existing independent complaints/supervisor route and explain it clearly. A pilot cannot certify regulatory compliance.

### Facilitation and assessment
Run a simulated review panel in which a participant representative can challenge assumptions. Assess ownership, evidence requirements, safeguards and willingness to stop. No learners should access real records or change real permissions. Extension: ask how the recommendation changes if efficiency improves but people using non-digital channels experience longer waits.

## Tutor review and use

These are original educational scenarios, not extracts from external curricula. No external primary references were verified for this batch. Before formal delivery, add current primary sources appropriate to the jurisdiction, qualification specification and organisation's approved policies; record source dates and distinguish mandatory requirements from teaching guidance. Care tutors should review terminology, safeguarding routes and information-handling examples locally. Assess answers against reasoning and the stated fictional facts; accept justified alternatives. Any use in accredited provision requires separate authorised mapping and quality assurance.
