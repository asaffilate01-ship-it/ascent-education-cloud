# UniPathway: Accountancy and separate ACCA starter lessons

Version: 19 November 2026. Status: original teaching drafts for tutor review, not accredited course content or an official examination paper.

## Scope and delivery

Six complete starter lessons: four in a UniPathway accountancy sequence labelled L2–L5, followed by two separate ACCA study-support lessons. These sequence labels are proposed teaching levels, not claims of qualification approval, regulated credit, exemptions, admission eligibility or equivalence between AAT, OTHM and ACCA. A qualified subject lead must map content against the exact approved qualification and current specification before using it for assessed delivery. ACCA content is not part of the L2–L5 sequence and is not endorsed by ACCA.

All businesses and figures are fictional. Amounts use currency units (CU) so exercises do not imply UK or Pakistan tax rules. Ignore tax, interest and exchange differences unless stated. Work on paper or a blank spreadsheet, never a production accounting system. Each lesson includes a teaching explanation, model, independent exercise, worked solution and exit check. Tutors should release solutions after the first attempt and assess reasoning as well as totals. A suggested 70% practice threshold signals readiness for further practice only, not a qualification pass.

## UP-ACC-L2-01: From business transactions to a balanced trial balance

**Time:** 60 minutes. **Prerequisites:** addition, subtraction and the difference between business and owner. **Reference:** R1, broad foundation sequencing only.

### Outcomes

Identify assets, liabilities, capital, income and expenses; explain debit and credit as ledger sides; record six transactions; construct a trial balance and explain its limits.

### Teaching explanation (15 minutes)

The accounting equation is assets = liabilities + equity. Assets are resources controlled by the business; liabilities are obligations. Owner investment is capital, not sales income. Profit increases equity; drawings reduce equity but are not business expenses.

In double entry, total debits equal total credits for each transaction. Debit asset increases and expense increases. Credit liability increases, capital increases and income increases. Reverse the direction for decreases. Debit does not always mean money received: buying equipment for cash debits equipment but credits bank. Credit sales debit receivables because the customer owes the business.

A source document helps establish date, amount, purpose and counterparty. Check an invoice against delivery evidence and authorisation before posting. Never create an invented invoice to make a ledger agree.

### Model (10 minutes)

A tutor contributes CU5,000 to a new business bank account: debit Bank 5,000; credit Capital 5,000. The business pays CU600 rent for the current month: debit Rent expense 600; credit Bank 600. Bank is now 4,400 debit; rent 600 debit; capital 5,000 credit. Trial-balance totals are 5,000 each. The equation is bank 4,400 = capital 5,000 less loss 600.

### Independent exercise (20 minutes; 10 marks)

A new fictional design studio starts with no balances. Post these transactions and prepare a trial balance:

1. Owner invests CU12,000 into bank.
2. Buy equipment for CU3,000, paying from bank.
3. Complete services for CU2,400 on credit.
4. Collect CU1,800 from that customer.
5. Pay current-month rent CU700 from bank.
6. Receive a CU300 utilities bill for this month, unpaid at month-end.

Allocate 6 marks to the six correct balanced entries, 2 to the trial balance, 1 to profit and 1 to explaining why a balanced trial balance does not prove complete accuracy.

### Worked answer

1. Dr Bank 12,000; Cr Capital 12,000.
2. Dr Equipment 3,000; Cr Bank 3,000.
3. Dr Receivables 2,400; Cr Service income 2,400.
4. Dr Bank 1,800; Cr Receivables 1,800.
5. Dr Rent 700; Cr Bank 700.
6. Dr Utilities 300; Cr Payables 300.

Bank = 12,000 − 3,000 + 1,800 − 700 = 10,100. Receivables = 2,400 − 1,800 = 600.

| Account | Debit CU | Credit CU |
|---|---:|---:|
| Bank | 10,100 | |
| Equipment | 3,000 | |
| Receivables | 600 | |
| Rent | 700 | |
| Utilities | 300 | |
| Capital | | 12,000 |
| Service income | | 2,400 |
| Payables | | 300 |
| **Total** | **14,700** | **14,700** |

Profit = 2,400 − 700 − 300 = 1,400. Equipment is an asset, not an immediate expense in this simplified exercise; depreciation is excluded. Assets 13,700 = liabilities 300 + capital 12,000 + profit 1,400. A completely omitted transaction or posting to the wrong expense account can still leave totals equal.

**Exit check:** Does collecting a receivable create income again? No: it exchanges receivable for bank. **Remediation:** if entry 4 is wrong, redraw the accounting equation before repeating the exercise with different amounts.

## UP-ACC-L3-01: Accruals and prepayments at year-end

**Time:** 60 minutes. **Prerequisite:** L2 double entry. **Reference:** R1, broad intermediate sequencing only.

### Outcomes

Separate cash paid from expense incurred; calculate prepaid and accrued amounts; post adjustments; explain their effect on profit and financial position.

### Teaching explanation (15 minutes)

An expense belongs to the period receiving the service, not necessarily the period paying cash. An accrual records service already consumed but not yet recorded as payable. A prepayment is a payment for a future service and is an asset until that service is consumed. For straightforward recurring expenses:

Expense = cash paid + closing accrual − opening accrual + opening prepayment − closing prepayment.

Use this equation only after identifying what the cash and balances represent. Do not blindly apply it to asset purchases. Keep a schedule showing invoice dates, coverage periods, calculations and reviewer approval.

### Model (10 minutes)

A business pays CU1,200 on 1 October for insurance covering twelve months to 30 September. Its year ends 31 December. Three months belong to this year: 1,200 × 3/12 = 300 expense. Nine months remain: 900 prepayment. If all 1,200 was initially expensed, adjust Dr Prepaid insurance 900; Cr Insurance expense 900. Bank does not change during this adjustment.

### Independent exercise (20 minutes; 10 marks)

A business has a 31 December year-end. Opening accruals and prepayments are zero. It recorded all cash payments below as expenses:

- Insurance paid 1 September: CU2,400 for twelve months from that date.
- Electricity paid and expensed in the year: CU3,600. An additional CU450 December bill has not been recorded or paid.
- Draft profit before these adjustments is CU8,000.

Calculate both final expense balances, closing accrual/prepayment, journals and adjusted profit. Explain why neither adjustment changes bank. Marks: calculations 4; journals 4; profit 1; explanation 1.

### Worked answer

Insurance expense = 2,400 × 4/12 = 800. Future eight months = 1,600 prepaid. Dr Prepaid insurance 1,600; Cr Insurance expense 1,600.

Electricity expense = 3,600 + 450 = 4,050. Dr Electricity expense 450; Cr Accrued electricity liability 450.

Adjusted profit = 8,000 + 1,600 − 450 = 9,150. Closing financial-position balances include prepaid insurance asset 1,600 and electricity liability 450. These adjustments recognise timing differences; cash payments were already recorded. Paying the electricity bill next period settles the liability and must not duplicate the expense.

**Exit check:** What if the December electricity bill was already posted to payables? Do not accrue it again. **Remediation:** draw a September-to-August timeline and shade only months before year-end.

## UP-ACC-L4-01: Flexible budgets and a fair performance comparison

**Time:** 70 minutes. **Prerequisites:** percentages, cost classification and basic spreadsheet formulas. **Reference:** R1, broad advanced sequencing only.

### Outcomes

Distinguish fixed and variable costs; flex a budget to actual activity; calculate cost variances consistently; propose evidence-based follow-up rather than blame.

### Teaching explanation (15 minutes)

A fixed budget uses planned output. Comparing it directly with actual costs can penalise a manager simply for producing more units. A flexible budget restates expected variable cost at actual activity, while fixed costs stay unchanged within the relevant activity range.

Flexible cost = variable cost per unit × actual units + budgeted fixed cost.

For this lesson, actual cost minus flexible cost is an adverse variance if positive and favourable if negative. A favourable cost variance is not automatically good: cheaper materials might increase faults. Check output quality, supplier changes and resource usage before reaching conclusions. These are aggregate spending comparisons, not a full materials price/usage variance analysis.

### Model (10 minutes)

At 1,000 units, variable cost is CU8 each and fixed cost CU5,000: budget 13,000. At actual 1,200 units, flexible budget is 9,600 + 5,000 = 14,600. Actual cost 14,900 is CU300 adverse against the flexible budget, not CU1,900 adverse solely attributable to spending performance.

### Independent exercise (25 minutes; 10 marks)

A fictional workshop planned 500 training kits. Budget: materials CU20 per kit; labour CU12 per kit; monthly fixed overhead CU6,000. Actual output was 600 kits. Actual materials cost 12,900; labour 6,900; fixed overhead 6,200. No inventory changes; costs are within the relevant range.

Prepare original and flexible budgets, calculate each variance and total, reconcile the total difference from the original budget, and suggest two investigations. Marks: budgets 4; variances 3; reconciliation 1; investigations 2.

### Worked answer

| Cost | Original CU | Flexible CU | Actual CU | Variance |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Materials | 10,000 | 12,000 | 12,900 | 900 adverse |
| Labour | 6,000 | 7,200 | 6,900 | 300 favourable |
| Fixed overhead | 6,000 | 6,000 | 6,200 | 200 adverse |
| **Total** | **22,000** | **25,200** | **26,000** | **800 adverse** |

Actual minus original = 4,000. Activity effect = (600 − 500) × (20 + 12) = 3,200; spending difference = 800. Therefore 4,000 = 3,200 + 800.

Investigate material supplier prices and scrap quantities using purchase and production records. Compare labour hours, pay rates and rejected kits: favourable labour cost could reflect efficiency, cheaper staffing or unfinished work. Also review the fixed-cost overrun against invoices before declaring it avoidable.

**Exit check:** Should fixed overhead automatically rise 20% because output rose 20%? No, not within the stated relevant range. **Extension:** identify the extra data needed to distinguish material price from usage effects.

## UP-ACC-L5-01: Evaluate a small investment using cash flows and risk

**Time:** 75 minutes. **Prerequisites:** cash versus profit, percentages and discount factors. **Reference:** R2, financial-management subject context; not a full unit mapping.

### Outcomes

Identify incremental project cash flows; calculate net present value (NPV); test a downside case; write a conditional recommendation supported by non-financial evidence.

### Teaching explanation (20 minutes)

NPV discounts future incremental cash flows to a common date and subtracts the initial outlay. Future CU1 is worth less today when a positive discount rate applies. Present value at the end of year t = cash flow / (1 + rate)^t. Accepting a positive-NPV project is a financial starting point, not automatic approval.

Exclude sunk costs already irreversibly incurred. Include relevant opportunity costs and future cash flows that change because of the decision. Depreciation is not itself a cash flow; real appraisals may include tax effects, but this exercise excludes tax. Do not subtract loan interest again when the chosen discount rate already reflects financing cost. Forecast uncertainty, capacity, accessibility, supplier reliability and maintenance all matter.

### Model (10 minutes)

Pay CU10,000 today for a project returning CU11,500 in one year. At 10%, present value = 11,500/1.10 = 10,454.55. NPV = 454.55. This is positive under the assumed cash flow and rate; it is not a guaranteed return.

### Independent exercise (25 minutes; 10 marks)

A fictional training centre considers equipment costing CU20,000 now. Net incremental operating cash inflows are CU8,000 at each year-end for three years. Year 3 also includes CU2,000 disposal proceeds. A CU1,000 feasibility report has already been paid and cannot be recovered. Discount rate 10%; no tax, inflation or working-capital changes.

Calculate base NPV; explain treatment of the report; calculate NPV if operating inflows are 20% lower but disposal unchanged; recommend next steps. Marks: base calculation 4; sunk-cost treatment 1; downside 3; recommendation 2.

### Worked answer

Use exact factors or four-decimal factors consistently; small rounding differences are acceptable.

- Year 1 PV: 8,000/1.1 = 7,272.73.
- Year 2 PV: 8,000/1.1² = 6,611.57.
- Year 3 PV: 10,000/1.1³ = 7,513.15.
- Base NPV = 21,397.45 − 20,000 = **CU1,397.45 positive**.

The feasibility report is sunk and excluded from the go-forward investment comparison. It remains a recorded cost in the business accounts.

Downside operating inflows are 6,400 a year. Present value = 6,400/1.1 + 6,400/1.1² + 8,400/1.1³ = 5,818.18 + 5,289.26 + 6,311.04 = 17,418.48. Downside NPV = **CU2,581.52 negative**.

Recommendation: the base case supports further consideration, but modest forecast deterioration reverses the result. Verify demand evidence, equipment uptime, maintenance obligations and disposal value; check cash availability and alternatives. Submit a documented recommendation for authorised review rather than treating positive base NPV as permission to purchase.

**Exit check:** Can a profitable accounting project face a cash shortage? Yes: receipt/payment timing and initial funding needs can differ from profit recognition. **Extension:** explain how delayed year-one receipts would affect PV without recalculating every year.

These are original supplementary practice lessons for the dated FA/FFA and MA/FMA syllabuses in R3/R4. They do not claim complete exam coverage, approved-provider status or ACCA exemptions. Check the learner’s actual exam and session before assignment, especially when syllabuses change.

## UP-ACCA-FA-01: Bank reconciliation and correcting the cash book

**Time:** 65 minutes. **Prerequisites:** debits, credits, bank asset balances. **Reference:** R3, reconciliation topic.

### Outcomes

Separate missing cash-book entries from bank timing differences; update the cash book; reconcile to the bank statement; identify evidence to investigate an unexplained difference.

### Teaching explanation (15 minutes)

The business cash book and bank statement can differ without either being wrong. Payments recorded by the business but not yet processed by the bank are outstanding payments. Receipts recorded by the business but not yet credited by the bank are deposits in transit. Neither normally requires a second cash-book posting.

Bank fees, direct debits and direct customer transfers may first become visible on the statement and require cash-book updates. Correct cash-book errors before preparing the reconciliation. A bank error requires evidence and follow-up with the bank, not an invented business transaction. For a positive bank-statement balance:

Adjusted cash book = statement balance + deposits in transit − outstanding payments.

This formula uses the business’s positive-asset perspective. Take extra care with overdrafts and signs.

### Model (10 minutes)

Cash book bank asset CU2,000 omits a bank charge CU25. Dr Bank charges 25; Cr Bank 25, giving 1,975. Statement balance is 2,275 and a CU300 supplier payment is outstanding. Reconciliation: 2,275 − 300 = 1,975. Do not record the supplier payment a second time.

### Independent exercise (25 minutes; 10 marks)

Cash book shows CU4,850 debit. Statement shows CU5,245 positive. Findings:

- CU35 bank fee not in cash book.
- CU600 direct customer receipt not in cash book; it settles an existing receivable.
- CU220 supplier payment recorded incorrectly as CU202 in cash book. It settles an existing payable; the bank processed CU220 correctly.
- Deposit in transit CU900.
- Outstanding supplier payments CU748.

Update the cash book with journals, reconcile, and explain treatment of timing items. Marks: journals 4; adjusted balance 2; reconciliation 2; explanation 2.

### Worked answer

Dr Bank charges 35; Cr Bank 35. Dr Bank 600; Cr Receivables 600. The supplier payment is understated by 220 − 202 = 18: Dr Payables 18; Cr Bank 18.

Adjusted cash-book bank = 4,850 − 35 + 600 − 18 = **5,397 debit**.

Statement reconciliation: 5,245 + 900 − 748 = **5,397**. Deposit and outstanding payments were already recorded correctly in the cash book; no new entries are made for them. Check subsequent statements for clearance and investigate unusually old items rather than deleting them automatically.

**Exit check:** Is the CU600 receipt new sales revenue? No: it clears an existing receivable. **Remediation:** classify each item as missing entry, error or timing difference before calculating.

## UP-ACCA-MA-01: Absorption costing and fixed production overhead

**Time:** 70 minutes. **Prerequisites:** direct/indirect and fixed/variable costs. **Reference:** R4, costing-technique topic.

### Outcomes

Calculate a predetermined overhead absorption rate; calculate unit production cost; identify under/over absorption; explain the profit effect of an inventory increase under explicit simplifying assumptions.

### Teaching explanation (20 minutes)

Absorption costing attaches production overhead to units using a chosen activity base. A predetermined rate uses budgeted production overhead divided by budgeted activity. For a fixed-overhead rate per unit:

Rate = budgeted fixed production overhead / budgeted units.

Absorbed overhead = rate × actual units produced. If actual overhead exceeds absorbed overhead, overhead is under-absorbed; if absorbed exceeds actual, it is over-absorbed. Non-production selling and administration costs are not part of production inventory cost in this exercise.

Marginal costing values inventory using variable production costs only and treats fixed production overhead as a period cost. Where there is no opening inventory, rates are constant and other assumptions match, absorption profit minus marginal profit equals inventory increase × fixed production overhead rate per unit. This is a costing comparison, not permission to overproduce to inflate reported performance.

### Model (10 minutes)

Budgeted fixed production overhead CU12,000 for 3,000 units gives CU4 per unit. Actual production 2,800 absorbs CU11,200. If actual fixed production overhead is CU11,700, CU500 is under-absorbed. A period profit adjustment expenses that shortfall under the simplified approach used here.

### Independent exercise (25 minutes; 10 marks)

A fictional manufacturer budgets 4,000 units and CU24,000 fixed production overhead. Variable production cost is CU14 per unit. Actual production is 4,200 units; sales 3,900 units at CU30 each. Actual fixed production overhead is CU25,800. Opening inventory is zero; no work in progress; all units have identical costs; there are no selling/admin costs. Apply the predetermined fixed rate and charge any under-absorption to current-period profit.

Find the rate, full production cost per unit, absorbed overhead, under/over absorption, closing inventory under both methods and both profits. Marks: rate/unit cost 2; absorption 2; inventory 2; profits and reconciliation 4.

### Worked answer

Rate = 24,000/4,000 = **CU6 per unit**. Full production cost = 14 + 6 = **CU20**. Absorbed fixed overhead = 4,200 × 6 = 25,200. Actual 25,800 is higher, so **CU600 under-absorbed**.

Closing inventory = 4,200 − 3,900 = 300 units. Absorption inventory = 300 × 20 = **6,000**; marginal inventory = 300 × 14 = **4,200**.

Revenue = 3,900 × 30 = 117,000. Absorption cost of sales before adjustment = 3,900 × 20 = 78,000. Absorption profit = 117,000 − 78,000 − 600 = **38,400**.

Marginal variable cost of sales = 3,900 × 14 = 54,600. Contribution = 117,000 − 54,600 = 62,400. Marginal profit = 62,400 − 25,800 = **36,600**.

Difference = 38,400 − 36,600 = **1,800**, equal to inventory increase 300 × CU6. Fixed production overhead of CU1,800 is carried in closing inventory under absorption costing instead of expensed immediately under marginal costing. Neither method changes cash received or paid.

**Exit check:** Does producing more inventory necessarily create more cash? No; unsold inventory can consume cash. **Extension:** explain why constant-rate assumptions matter if opening inventory was produced in another period.
