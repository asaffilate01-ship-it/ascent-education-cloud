# School tuition: Wave 2

**Staff review only.** These 12 original micro-lessons are for formative teaching. Each suggested route is a review lane, not a verified syllabus or exam alignment. Confirm the board, subject, grade or exam tier, entry/session, official outcome and language before mapping. Keep routes separate even where a skill overlaps. All scenarios are fictional; use paper or a local scratch tool, not real personal records or practical apparatus.

## W2-SCH-01 · Reconstruct a missing frequency

**Audience / route:** Grade 8 mathematics; Punjab route proposed.  
**Objective:** Recover an unknown category count from totals, then interpret the fraction of the group it represents.  
**Worked example:** Of 40 library visitors, 11 chose fiction, 9 chose history and the rest chose science. Science accounts for 40−11−9=20 visitors, or 20/40=1/2. This assumes each visitor chose one category.  
**Independent practice:** A 50-response class poll records 16 votes for cricket and 19 for badminton. Every pupil chose exactly one option; the third option is football. Find the football count and percentage and write one conclusion the poll does not support.  
**Key:** 15 votes; 30%. It does not support a claim about pupils in other schools.  
**Progression:** Draw a frequency bar chart, then explain why percentages may not add to 100 if a new poll allows multiple responses.

## W2-SCH-02 · Analyse prices after a fixed fee

**Audience / route:** Grade 9 mathematics; Punjab route proposed.  
**Objective:** Interpret a fixed fee and per-unit charge in a linear model and compare two tariffs.  
**Worked example:** Two fictional printers quote Rs 120 plus Rs 6 per page and Rs 60 plus Rs 9 per page. At 20 pages both cost Rs 240. Each extra page after 20 increases the first bill by Rs 3 less than the second bill.  
**Independent practice:** Compare A: Rs 80 plus Rs 7 per copy, and B: Rs 140 plus Rs 4 per copy. Find the break-even number of copies and the cheaper option at 32 copies, with the difference.  
**Key:** Equal at 20 copies, Rs 220 each. At 32 copies A is Rs 304 and B is Rs 268; B is cheaper by Rs 36.  
**Progression:** Plot both graphs and explain the meaning and units of their intercepts and gradients.

## W2-SCH-03 · Link a source’s purpose to its usefulness

**Audience / route:** Grade 10 Pakistan Studies / English literacy; Punjab route proposed.  
**Objective:** Evaluate which fictional source can answer a narrow historical question and identify what remains unproven.  
**Worked example:** A town council’s dated invoice can support a claim that it paid for notice printing, but not that notices reached every household; a delivery list answers a different question and can itself be incomplete.  
**Independent practice:** Source A is a dated meeting resolution authorising a footbridge. Source B is a receipt for twenty bags of cement. Which best supports approval and which supports a purchase? What record would establish that construction was completed?  
**Key:** A supports approval; B supports cement purchase, not use or completion. A signed completion certificate or an independently dated completion record would help verify completion.  
**Progression:** Compare a fictional eyewitness account with the official record and explain how independent evidence can corroborate it.

## W2-SCH-04 · Compare friction in a distance trial

**Audience / route:** Grade 9 physics; Sindh route proposed.  
**Objective:** Use repeated measurements to compare surfaces and explain what a controlled trial needs.  
**Worked example:** A classroom simulation models a cart launched equally on two surfaces. Travel distances on A are 78, 80 and 82 cm; on B they are 48, 50 and 52 cm. Their means are 80 and 50 cm. The shorter distance is consistent with greater opposing force, provided the starting speed and other conditions match.  
**Independent practice:** Given X: 63, 66, 69 cm and Y: 41, 44, 47 cm, find both means. Explain the confound if X started from a steeper ramp.  
**Key:** X=66 cm; Y=44 cm. A steeper ramp could change starting speed, so the difference cannot confidently be attributed to surface friction alone.  
**Progression:** Use ranges to describe measurement spread without treating three observations as a universal rule.

## W2-SCH-05 · Track the same sample before and after drying

**Audience / route:** Grade 10 chemistry/data skills; Sindh route proposed.  
**Objective:** Subtract a container’s mass before calculating the mass fraction lost in a hypothetical drying process.  
**Worked example:** Empty dish 15.0 g, dish plus wet sample 30.0 g, dish plus dry sample 25.5 g. Initial sample is 15.0 g and loss is 4.5 g, or 30%. Calling all lost mass water requires assuming no solids or other volatiles escaped.  
**Independent practice:** Use fictional readings 20.0 g empty, 44.0 g wet and 38.0 g dry. Find dry sample mass and percentage lost from the starting sample.  
**Key:** Dry sample 18.0 g; 6.0 g out of 24.0 g was lost, or 25%. Do not divide by the dish-and-sample mass.  
**Progression:** Compare two sample masses using percentages, and describe why weighing to constant mass is valuable in supervised inquiry. This is data interpretation only.

## W2-SCH-06 · Find the downstream effect of a reservoir change

**Audience / route:** Grade 11 geography / environmental science enrichment; Sindh route proposed.  
**Objective:** Construct a cause-and-effect chain and name measurable evidence for testing it.  
**Worked example:** A fictional dry month lowers river flow; reduced freshwater discharge can make it easier for seawater to move upstream near the mouth. Pair flow readings with salinity at fixed locations and similar tidal stages before drawing a conclusion.  
**Independent practice:** Upstream salinity readings on two equal-tide days are 0.8 and 1.3 g/L, while nearer the mouth they are 7.0 and 7.2 g/L. Report changes, then propose one alternative explanation besides river flow.  
**Key:** Upstream change +0.5 g/L; near-mouth +0.2 g/L. Rainfall distribution, sampling timing or changed upstream withdrawals could affect readings; one comparison is not proof of cause.  
**Progression:** Sketch repeat sampling at three locations and explain why a fixed timing and measurement procedure improve comparability.

## W2-SCH-07 · Read wave speed from a diagram

**Audience / route:** Grade 10 physics; Federal Board route proposed.  
**Objective:** Obtain wavelength from several intervals, distinguish it from amplitude and calculate speed from frequency.  
**Worked example:** On a displacement–distance sketch, first-to-fourth crest spans 1.20 m. Three intervals give wavelength 0.40 m. At 5 Hz the wave speed is 2.0 m/s. Vertical crest height above equilibrium is amplitude, not wavelength.  
**Independent practice:** The second-to-sixth crest spans 1.80 m and frequency is 8 Hz. Find wavelength and speed. State what a 0.05 m vertical offset from equilibrium to crest represents.  
**Key:** Four intervals, wavelength 0.45 m; speed 3.6 m/s; the vertical offset is amplitude.  
**Progression:** Add a displacement–time sketch at one position and identify the period independently of wavelength.

## W2-SCH-08 · Compare interval slopes with unequal time windows

**Audience / route:** Grade 11 mathematics; Federal Board route proposed.  
**Objective:** Calculate and compare interval slopes on a nonlinear pattern without claiming instantaneous rates.  
**Worked example:** A fictional filling model gives volumes 0, 3 and 8 L at minutes 0, 1 and 2. The interval slopes are 3 and 5 L/min. The full two-minute average is 4 L/min even though increments are unequal.  
**Independent practice:** Record model volumes 6, 14 and 38 L at minutes 1, 3 and 7. Find the two adjacent interval rates and the overall rate. Explain why the overall rate is not the unweighted mean of the interval rates.  
**Key:** 4 and 6 L/min; overall 32/6=16/3 L/min. The second interval lasts four minutes, so it carries greater weight.  
**Progression:** Compare equal-duration intervals first, then link smaller secants to the rate at a point without assuming exact equality.

## W2-SCH-09 · Test whether a seedling sample represents a park

**Audience / route:** Grade 12 biology/data literacy; Federal Board route proposed.  
**Objective:** Distinguish a convenience sample from a stratified design in an observational study.  
**Worked example:** A study of seedling height records only a greenhouse’s bright front bench. To compare the whole greenhouse, list benches by light condition, randomly select seedlings within each condition and measure all at the same age.  
**Independent practice:** A park’s east and west zones each have 60 seedlings; a learner samples only 12 near the east entrance. Design a 12-seedling sample and name one limitation still present.  
**Key:** Randomly select six from each complete zone list; hold age, timing and measurement procedure as comparable as possible. Results may not generalise to other parks or seasons; the design is observational, not evidence of the zone causing any observed difference.  
**Progression:** Compare proportional allocation if the zones have unequal population sizes.

## W2-SCH-10 · Measure a return bearing

**Audience / route:** Upper-secondary Cambridge mathematics; exact syllabus code/tier/session mapping proposed.  
**Objective:** Use a north reference, clockwise three-figure angles and the 180° difference for reverse bearings.  
**Worked example:** If B is on a bearing of 070° from A, draw parallel north arrows at both locations. The reverse bearing is 070°+180°=250°, not 110°.  
**Independent practice:** Point Q lies on bearing 325° from P. Write the bearing of P from Q and the bearing of an eastward ray from Q, both in three figures.  
**Key:** 325°−180°=145°; east is 090°. Draw north arrows to keep the reference at the point of observation.  
**Progression:** Use a scale drawing to approximate the direct bearing after two non-collinear legs, then check it with coordinates.

## W2-SCH-11 · Select a test that distinguishes two rule models

**Audience / route:** Upper-secondary Cambridge computer science; 2210/0478 mapping not assigned.  
**Objective:** Compare ordered rules by choosing a case that makes their results differ, rather than rechecking cases both pass.  
**Worked example:** A room model assigns MORNING if arrival is before 12, otherwise AFTERNOON, but a second model first checks for a room closure and always returns CLOSED when needed. A closure at 11 gives different outputs; an open room at 11 does not.  
**Independent practice:** Model A applies a £0 replacement when an item is under warranty, otherwise charges £15. Model B first applies a condition override: accidental damage always costs £15, then checks warranty. Supply one differing case and a control case that produces the same charge.  
**Key:** Under warranty + accidental damage: A £0, B £15. No warranty with no accidental damage is a shared £15 control; under warranty with no accidental damage is a shared £0 control.  
**Progression:** Write a complete four-row decision table and use it to justify which condition must have priority. This is a rule comparison, not guidance about any real warranty.

## W2-SCH-12 · Compare two descriptions of the same place

**Audience / route:** Upper-secondary Cambridge English; exact first-language/second-language course mapping proposed.  
**Objective:** Analyse how selection of concrete details and sentence rhythm creates two impressions without changing core facts.  
**Worked example:** “The hall filled; chairs scraped; the doors shut.” Short clauses make the sequence feel brisk. “As the hall filled, chairs slid slowly across the floor before the doors finally shut” slows it; the same hall fills and the doors close.  
**Independent practice:** Compare “A lamp glowed in the rain; the shop waited” with “A lamp flickered in the rain. The shop was empty.” Identify one common fact and show how one specific verb alters the mood.  
**Key:** Both describe a lamp and rain around a shop. “Glowed” suggests steadiness or warmth; “flickered” suggests instability or unease. A well-supported alternative emotional reading is acceptable.  
**Progression:** Rewrite both descriptions for a noticeboard report, retaining shared facts but removing personification and mood-setting phrasing.
