## Workshop 06 | Design a fairer queue trial

**Outcome focus:** 1.3, 2.1–2.3 and 3.4. Learners distinguish measures from assumptions and build a method another observer could repeat.

**0–10 min: Diagnose the original sample.** Contrast Monday’s one-till shift (32 timings, 10-minute median) with Tuesday’s two-till shift (14 timings, 6-minute median). Which detail changes alongside the till count? Why is “customers timed” not the same as total customer arrivals?

**10–25 min: Build a protocol.** Define the primary measure as minutes from joining the line to placing the order. Record queue abandonments separately. Compare matched weekday lunch periods across several weeks; alternate conditions where operationally practical. Use the same sampling rule and time window for each shift. Log total arrivals, number timed, staff mix and major service interruptions. No names, footage or customer contact details.

**25–45 min: Rehearse with this fictional log.** The first five timed completions took 4, 6, 7, 7 and 11 minutes. A sixth person left after waiting eight minutes. Calculate the median for the five completed waits and report the abandonment separately. Explain how you would keep the group comparison fair if departures were more common in one condition.

**45–60 min: Swap, test and revise.** A partner reads your method as if they are the next observer. Mark each unclear instruction. Revise the rules covering start time, end time, missed observations and departures.

**Independent practice (60 min):** Write a one-page protocol and a blank observation sheet. Include the comparison, one measure, one potential confounder and how you will handle missing or incomplete observations.

**Lecturer check:** The five completed waits have a median of seven minutes. Do not add the abandoned wait to that median as though it were a completed order. Reporting only the completed median may hide frustration, so show departures and the relevant denominator separately.

## Workshop 07 | Draft and revise a research briefing

**Outcome focus:** 1.1–1.3, 2.1–2.3 and 3.1–3.4. Learners use sources for different purposes and explain why their conclusion is limited.

**0–10 min: Compare two claims.** “A second till reduced waiting by four minutes” overstates what two different shifts can prove. “Tuesday’s observed median was four minutes lower than Monday’s, but the shifts differed in other ways” reports what the practice log supports.

**10–25 min: Build an evidence grid.** For each of the café timing log, sales export, supplier advertisement and outside practice study, record its owner/date or record ID, what it measures, what it does not measure, and how it can contribute. Explain which source was collected for this question and which already existed. Use the starter pack’s source cards; there is no need to invent actual publication details.

**25–45 min: Draft one section.** Choose either your proposed method or a critical comparison of two sources. Write 150–200 words with a clear claim, specific evidence, a limitation and a consequence for how the result should be used. Use a consistent reference to the relevant practice record.

**45–60 min: Peer edit.** Label unsupported claims, unclear references and wording that implies causation. Ask the writer to revise the weakest inference and identify what evidence would change their view.

**Independent practice (90 min):** Write a 600–800-word practice briefing with four sections: question and approach, evidence and method, evaluation of the argument, and a cautious recommendation. Add an evidence log and a short revision note. This shorter task prepares learners for coursework without substituting for it.

**Lecturer check:** A strong response explains why the method fits the question, compares two sources against the same use, and names a plausible alternative explanation. It does not confuse a supplier claim with independent evidence or use sample size as a substitute for demand.

## Workshop 08 | Give a ten-minute evidence-led presentation

**Outcome focus:** 4.1–4.3. Learners build an argument with a premise, evidence and a conclusion, and demonstrate the difference between inference from a sample and deduction from a rule.

**0–10 min: Model the structure.** Use the four-shift timing table in the starter pack. Show two statements: “The two-till shifts looked faster in this small set, so the pattern warrants a trial” (inductive); “If the policy is to investigate a median over eight minutes, and Thursday’s median was nine, Thursday meets the investigation threshold” (deductive). Neither claim establishes that opening a second till is the cause.

**10–20 min: Make four slides or four cue cards.**
1. **1 minute:** State the decision, audience and recommendation.
2. **3 minutes:** Show all four shifts. Label the unit (minutes), median and number timed. Attribute the fictional log; explain what was measured.
3. **3 minutes:** Compare both forms of reasoning. State one alternative explanation and a weakness in the evidence.
4. **3 minutes:** Propose a fair trial, explain what would change your recommendation, and close with the limit of your conclusion.

**20–48 min: Rehearse in pairs.** Each learner presents for ten minutes, then receives four minutes of feedback. The observer records when the main argument, both reasoning examples and the conclusion appeared.

**48–60 min: Revise.** Improve one slide and practise the final sentence. Use readable labels and describe any chart aloud. For online rehearsal use live audio or the centre’s approved accessible equivalent; do not require a recording for this practice.

**Independent practice (60 min):** Revise the slides or cards, do one timed run and note one change that improved the argument.
