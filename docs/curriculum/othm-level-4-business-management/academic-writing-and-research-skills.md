## Lesson 01 | Turn a business problem into a research question
**60 minutes · OTHM alignment: 1.1–1.3**

**By the end:** Define the purpose of academic research and writing in your own words, choose a research approach, and rewrite an opinion as a claim you could test.

**Teach (15 min).** Academic research starts with an answerable question and works systematically with evidence. Academic writing lets someone see how you reached the answer and where it might be wrong. Keep the tone precise, signpost the argument, credit your sources and distinguish evidence from opinion. Descriptive research asks what is happening; associational research asks which things vary together; intervention research tests what changes after a planned change.

**Worked example (10 min).** “Our queues are awful” does not tell the café what to measure. “What is the median wait from joining the queue to placing an order between noon and 2 p.m. on weekdays?” defines a measure, a group and a period. Asking whether busier periods have longer waits is associational. Testing a second till on matched shifts is an intervention.

**Workshop (25 min).** In pairs, write a descriptive, associational and intervention question for the café. For each, name the measure and one limitation. Swap work with another pair and ask whether a stranger could collect the same measure. Online: post one revised question in the class discussion; in-person: compare on paper.

**Exit check (10 min).** In 80 words, explain how you would test “extra staff will cut queues.” Give one reason why your result might not apply outside lunch.

## Lesson 02 | Find, compare and record evidence
**60 minutes · OTHM alignment: 2.1–2.3**

**By the end:** Distinguish primary and secondary research, compare two sources for an actual decision and make a short plan someone else could follow.

**Teach (15 min).** Primary research collects new evidence for your question, such as fresh timed observations. Secondary research reuses evidence that already exists, such as a published study or last month’s café records. A source can be credible without being directly relevant. Record its author or owner, date, method, sample, purpose and limitations. Start with the question, plan the method, collect only what you need, analyse it, then report and review limitations.

**Worked example (10 min).** A newly timed lunch queue measures waiting directly, but two days may not represent a normal week. Existing till timestamps cover more days but show completed sales, not when anyone joined the queue. Neither source alone can establish whether opening a second till caused a change.

**Workshop (25 min).** Use records A–D in the worksheet. Choose the best pair for (a) describing the lunch wait and (b) planning a second-till trial. For each choice, state what the source can and cannot show. Write a method with a time window, sampling plan and a measure you can collect without identifying customers.

**Exit check (10 min).** Which is stronger for estimating the local wait: a peer-reviewed study in another country or the café’s four days of observations? State which question each can answer well.

## Lesson 03 | Test an argument rather than just disagreeing
**60 minutes · OTHM alignment: 3.1–3.4**

**By the end:** Separate a claim, its evidence and its assumptions; identify an alternative explanation; revise a conclusion to fit the evidence.

**Teach (15 min).** Critical thinking is the habit of examining how well a conclusion follows from what we know. Ask whether the evidence is relevant, whether the measure fits the question, what is missing, and what else could have produced the same result. Treat a plausible alternative as a question to investigate, not as proof that the original claim is false.

**Worked example (10 min).** A manager says “Waits fell on Tuesday because we opened a second till.” A rival explanation is lower demand: the sample on Tuesday was much smaller. Compare matched time windows and record staffing, order volume and any unusual events before drawing a causal conclusion.

**Workshop (25 min).** Use the four-shift table. Identify one fact, one inference and two plausible alternative explanations. Rewrite the manager’s claim in one cautious sentence. Exchange answers and challenge the strongest unsupported assumption.

**Exit check (10 min).** Name one piece of evidence that would weaken your preferred explanation and explain why.

## Lesson 04 | Build an evidence-led recommendation
**60 minutes · OTHM alignment: 4.1–4.3**

**By the end:** Explain the difference between inductive and deductive reasoning and structure an argument with evidence, a counterpoint and a proportionate conclusion.

**Teach (15 min).** A sound recommendation states the decision, supports it with relevant evidence, addresses a counterpoint and says what is still uncertain. Induction moves from observations to a provisional generalisation. In deduction, if the premises are true and the reasoning is valid, the conclusion follows; the premises may still need checking.

**Worked example (10 min).** Induction: “The two-till shifts in our small sample had shorter median waits; a wider trial may show whether this pattern holds.” Deduction: “If the agreed rule says to investigate any shift with a median over eight minutes, and Thursday’s median was nine minutes, Thursday meets the rule.” The threshold is hypothetical; the second statement does not show two tills are necessary.

**Workshop (25 min).** Outline a short recommendation: question → claim → evidence → alternative explanation → next step. Write a 150-word argument. Justify a matched-shift trial or a different next step, rather than claiming the table proves causation.

**Exit check (10 min).** Underline the sentence in your draft that limits the conclusion. If you do not have one, add it.

## Lesson 05 | Cite, paraphrase and revise
**60 minutes · Reinforces 1.2, 2.1, 3.4 and 4.3**

**By the end:** Paraphrase without distorting the source, reference an evidence claim consistently, and improve a paragraph after peer feedback.

**Teach (15 min).** Read the source, put it aside, write the point in your own structure, then check it against the original. Cite borrowed ideas and data even when you change the wording. Follow the centre’s referencing style. Keep a simple log: title, author, date, link or internal record ID, and page or section.

**Worked example (10 min).** Opening hours and sample sizes are facts that do not need creative rewriting. What does need care is the inference: replace “two tills always fix the problem” with “the four-shift practice log shows shorter waits on the observed two-till shifts, but the comparison does not control for demand” and cite the worksheet record.

**Workshop (25 min).** Pair up. The first reader checks whether each evidence claim has a source; the second checks whether any wording overstates the finding. Revise the paragraph and add a change note with two edits and your reasons.

**Exit check (10 min).** Hand in the original and revised paragraphs plus two source log entries. This is practice, not a graded submission.

## Learner worksheet | Should the café open a second till?
**Source cards (fictional)**
- **A. UniPathway practice observation log.** Timed observations from queue entry to order placement on four noon-to-2 p.m. shifts. Approximate, with no customer identifiers.
- **B. Café till export.** Four weeks of sale timestamps. It records transactions but not queue entry, incomplete orders, or people who left.
- **C. Café supplier advertising email.** Says its till cuts waiting, but shows no measurement method.
- **D. Practice research summary.** A separate café ran a controlled comparison of two staffing arrangements; the shops, times and menu differ from the Jhelum scenario.

| Shift | Tills open | Customers timed | Median wait |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Monday | 1 | 32 | 10 min |
| Tuesday | 2 | 14 | 6 min |
| Wednesday | 2 | 18 | 7 min |
| Thursday | 1 | 30 | 9 min |

1. Write one answerable question and label the research approach.
2. Identify one primary observation and one secondary record. Compare A and B in terms of what they measure.
3. Using the table, write one claim it supports and one it does not.
4. Suggest a fairer trial. Give the measure, comparison, sampling period and one limit.
5. Recommend a next step in 120–150 words. Attribute the evidence and include a counterpoint.

## Lecturer notes | Indicators of a strong answer
- The median waits on the observed two-till shifts were lower, **but** those shifts also had fewer timed customers. The table shows a pattern, not why it happened. The number timed is not necessarily total demand; do not treat it as such.
- A directly records waits but is narrow; B gives operational context, not wait times. D can help design the trial, but its findings may not transfer. C is a conflicted source with no clear method.
- A defensible trial compares matched weekday lunch shifts across multiple weeks, varies till staffing where practical, and records demand consistently, using an anonymous timing method. Define the primary measure before collecting data and note potential changes in staff mix, menu or events.
- Give formative feedback on question clarity, fitness of evidence, reasoning, attribution and limits. Do not use this worksheet to award unit credit.
