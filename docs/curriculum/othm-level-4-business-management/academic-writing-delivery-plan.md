## Delivery and time

Each block budgets **10 guided hours** (2 hours of teaching, 3 hours of supervised workshop, 3 hours of supervised practice and 2 hours of coached feedback) and **10 independent hours** (3 reading or preparation, 4 project work and 3 revision or reflection). Ten blocks total **100 guided + 100 independent = 200 planned hours**. The eight one-hour drafts already written are included in the guided allocations, not added on top. Time boxes are planning estimates; replace them with verified session time in the delivery log. Classroom and synchronous online time can use the same sequence when active supervision is available. A recording, reading list or self-paced task alone does not count as guided delivery. Do not count the same activity twice.

## Workshop briefs for the additional guided time

Use each brief more than once only if the second session uses a new question, case or feedback goal. Scale group size and facilitation to the learners, not the planned hours. Ask each learner to retain one original output per supervised task.

1. **Question clinic (block 01):** The café owner wants to know whether a menu board would help. Have learners write a descriptive, an associational and an intervention question. The lecturer asks whether each question has a measurable outcome and a defined population. Extension: change the outcome from ordering time to order accuracy and check whether the method still fits.
2. **Source comparison lab (block 02):** Rank a brief observation log, a four-week sales export and a vendor claim for two different decisions. Swap rankings after clarifying the decision. There is no universal ordering: judge fitness for the specific question.
3. **Counterexample lab (block 03):** “The queue shortened after the menu changed; the menu must be responsible.” Groups diagram the inference and devise one observation that would weaken it. Possible alternatives include arrival volume, staffing and fewer large orders.
4. **Reasoning lab (block 04):** Compare a provisional claim drawn from three shifts with a deduction using an agreed investigation threshold. Ask whether the premises are true, whether the deduction follows and whether the claim overgeneralises. Rewrite the weaker argument.
5. **Attribution lab (block 05):** Rewrite a source-based paragraph without retaining its sentence structure. Swap drafts and trace each borrowed idea to a source log entry. Clarify that a citation does not excuse copied wording, invented evidence or an unacknowledged quotation.
6. **Observer consistency lab (block 06):** Two observers independently time the same scripted queue. Compare their definitions for entering the queue and completing an order. Re-run using agreed rules. Discuss whether reproducibility alone makes a measure valid.
7. **Editorial clinic (block 07):** Colour-code claim, evidence, reasoning and limitation in a briefing. Exchange the limitation paragraphs and check whether each one actually changes the recommendation or merely adds a caveat.
8. **Oral rehearsal lab (block 08):** A listener explains the chart back to the speaker without seeing it. If the result is wrong, revise the slide label or narration. Time the second attempt and keep one specific peer observation.
9. **Coursework clinic (block 09):** Fictional training centre case: a reminder email was introduced. Session A had 12 attendees out of 20 bookings; Session B had 15 out of 20. B was also moved to Saturday. A manager says reminders caused the improvement. Learners write an answerable question, compare the two records with a possible additional source, propose a fair test, and critique the claim in 300–400 words. **Facilitator check:** attendance rose from 60% to 75% (15 percentage points); the timing change prevents attribution to the email; two groups of bookings need not be identical.
10. **Oral argument clinic (block 10):** Use the training centre case to prepare four cue cards: decision, observed result, alternative explanation, and a proposed check. Speak for ten minutes. The observer records evidence for 4.1–4.3, then gives formative feedback. **Facilitator check:** “the observations suggest investigating reminders” is inductive; “if the centre’s rule is to review attendance below 70%, and A was at 60%, A triggers review” is deductive, conditional on the fictional rule. Neither proves the reminder worked.

## Assessment and quality controls

- **Coursework preparation:** The reference specification describes ~2,250 words across outcomes 1–3. The practice briefing and clinic are deliberately shorter. Have the programme lead verify the actual approved brief and separate the practice case from formal assessed evidence.
- **Presentation preparation:** The reference specification describes a ten-minute presentation for outcome 4. Use the observer card for practice. For a formal presentation, verify an approved private evidence route before assigning the activity. A PDF of the slides alone does not establish performance.
- **Roles:** Central content team versions the resources; lecturers teach, run workshops and give formative feedback; qualified assessors make formal decisions; IQA samples independently. Do not store learner work, recordings or grades in GitHub.
- **Review and release:** Confirm the awarding-body reference and exact live module, review the plan and all resources, check accessibility and rights, and verify draft visibility using an assigned lecturer plus enrolled and non-enrolled test learners before private release. Record the results separately; this plan does not authorise a live change.
