# English

## EN08-B — Narrative sequence and consistent tense
**Prerequisite:** EN08 evidence-based reading; simple past tense. **Objectives:** order events using time clues; maintain past tense; distinguish a character's belief from a narrator's fact. **Materials:** passage and six event cards.

**Original passage:** “When Aisha reached the classroom, a cardboard model lay beside the window. One wheel was missing. She remembered placing the model on a high shelf the previous afternoon. For a moment she blamed the wind. Then she noticed a muddy shoeprint on a sheet of paper and heard a scraping sound behind the cupboard. A small cat emerged, pushing the missing wheel with its paw. Aisha laughed, fetched a cloth and began to repair the model.”

**Teach:** A narrative can begin after an earlier event. “Had placed” could make the earlier time explicit, but “the previous afternoon” already signals it here. The wind is Aisha's initial explanation, not an established cause. Sequence events before polishing vocabulary. Model revision: “She sees the wheel and picked it up” → “She saw the wheel and picked it up.”

**Guided:** order these cards: repairs model; places model on shelf; hears scraping; arrives; notices print; cat emerges. Identify a clue that changes Aisha's understanding. Discuss why the shoeprint alone does not prove who damaged the model.

**Independent:** (1) Write the correct six-event order. (2) Correct “The cat appears and pushed the wheel.” (3) Write 100–120 words continuing the story, consistently in the past, including a setback, response and ending. (4) Underline two time links and one sentence describing a belief rather than a fact.

**Key:** shelf → arrives → notices print → hears scraping → cat emerges → repairs. “The cat appeared and pushed the wheel.” Accept coherent continuations without presenting them as original-passage facts. Sample opening: “After the cat disappeared, Aisha checked the axle. It was bent, so the wheel would not turn. She asked a classmate to hold the model while she straightened it.”
**Rubric /10:** sequence 2, tense 2, clear setback/response 2, cohesive links 2, readable sentences 2. **Exit:** explain why “Aisha knew the wind broke it” misrepresents the passage. **Support:** colour-code time references. **Stretch/homework:** retell from the cat's viewpoint while marking imagined details as fiction.

## EN09-B — Formal requests with a precise purpose
**Prerequisite:** EN09 claim/evidence reasoning. **Objectives:** write a respectful request; supply relevant logistics; distinguish a proposal from permission already granted.

**Case:** A fictional class wants to use a spare room for a weekly reading group. Twelve pupils have expressed interest, but attendance is not yet confirmed. Proposed time: Wednesdays 13:00–13:25 for four weeks. The room's availability and adult supervision are unknown. No budget is provided.

**Teach:** A formal request names the recipient's role, purpose, proposed arrangements and specific question. It does not demand approval or claim “everyone agrees.” Model: “Would it be possible to trial a reading group on Wednesdays? Twelve pupils have expressed interest. We would appreciate guidance on room availability and supervision before confirming any sessions.” Interest is not attendance; a proposed timetable is not a booking.

**Guided:** revise “Give us the room because everyone needs it. We start tomorrow.” Identify missing duration, supervision and approval. Discuss a respectful tone that remains clear rather than excessively apologetic.

**Independent:** write a 140–170-word request to the fictional year coordinator. Include purpose, accurate interest figure, dates as a four-week proposal, room/supervision questions, and a review after the trial. Then write a two-sentence response accepting a condition that the coordinator might impose. Do not invent real names or send the letter.

**Teacher model:** “Dear Year Coordinator, I am writing to ask whether our class could trial a lunchtime reading group for four weeks. Twelve pupils have expressed interest, although we have not yet confirmed attendance. We propose meeting on Wednesdays from 13:00 to 13:25. Could you advise whether a suitable room and approved supervision would be available? We would keep the room tidy and follow the school's rules. Before making arrangements, we would welcome any conditions you consider necessary. At the end of the trial, we could report attendance and ask participants whether the sessions were useful. Thank you for considering our request. Yours sincerely, Class Representative.” This shorter model demonstrates structure; learners still meet the practice word range.
**Rubric /10:** purpose 2, accurate logistics 2, unanswered questions 2, register 2, organisation/editing 2. **Exit:** replace one sentence implying permission with a genuine request. **Homework:** revise for a coordinator who has only thirty seconds to read it.

## EN10-B — Presenting a viewpoint and answering a question
**Suggested Grade 10 · Objective:** organise a brief talk for a specific audience, signpost two reasons and answer a challenge respectfully. **Retrieval:** distinguish a claim, supporting evidence and a recommendation.

**Scenario (original):** A school has one spare classroom for one 30-minute lunch club per week. In a voluntary response, 18 pupils requested a reading club and 12 requested a board-game club. No pupil was required to reply. Prepare a one-minute proposal for the school council.

**Model opening:** “I propose starting with a fortnightly trial that alternates reading and games. Reading was more popular among the 30 respondents, but a voluntary survey cannot tell us what the whole school wants.” Continue by explaining the 30-minute space limit, then ask the council to compare attendance across the first two sessions before choosing a permanent format. Signposts such as “first”, “however” and “so I propose” help a listener follow the reasoning.

**Guided task:** Rehearse with a partner. The listener lists the claim, one relevant figure, the survey limitation and the next step. Try again without reading the full text. If speaking is not accessible, use an audio recording, typed cue-card talk or signed presentation with the same criteria.

**Independent practice:** (1) Draft an opening no longer than 25 words. (2) Give a 45–60-second proposal with two reasons and a next step. (3) Respond to “Why not reserve the room exclusively for the majority?” **Example response:** “The majority of respondents chose reading, not necessarily the majority of pupils. A short trial gives us attendance evidence before we commit the room.” A different proposal is valid if the evidence and limits are not distorted.

**Formative check (4 marks):** clear viewpoint, accurate use of evidence, explicit caveat, responsive next step. **Exit:** name one difference between addressing classmates and making the case to the school council. Model: classmates may need an inviting tone; a council needs a feasible decision and evidence.

## EN11-B — Attribution and source-based paragraphs
**Prerequisite:** EN11 fair viewpoint comparison. **Objectives:** distinguish quotation/paraphrase/own analysis; attribute ideas; reconcile apparent disagreement without inventing agreement.

**Original fictional source notes:**
Source A, Community Library Committee report: “During the pilot, 18 of 24 reserved study desks were used on Saturday morning. The count covered one morning only.”
Source B, volunteer interview: “Some people asked for evening access, but we did not record how many. Evening opening would require another trained volunteer.”
These sources are invented classroom documents, not real research.

**Teach:** Attribution shows where a claim came from. A paraphrase changes wording but still needs attribution. A direct quotation must retain exact wording and quotation marks. Neither source establishes total community demand. Model: “The committee's single-morning count recorded use of 18 of 24 reserved desks. This suggests substantial use during that session, but it cannot establish demand for evening access.”

**Guided:** correct “Research proves most residents need evening opening.” Identify two unsupported changes: broader population and evening demand. Convert one source sentence into an accurate attributed paraphrase.

**Independent:** write a 160–190-word paragraph answering “What further information is needed before extending opening?” Use both sources, one short quotation, one paraphrase and clearly identified analysis. Add a brief source list using the given fictional titles. Do not fabricate publication dates or authors.

**Key:** 75% of reserved desks were used during one session, not 75% of residents; evening requests are uncounted; staffing is a constraint. Appropriate next evidence includes counts across different sessions and recorded preferences, gathered without unnecessary personal data. Model synthesis: “While the committee records use at a particular time, the interview identifies a possible unmet need that remains unmeasured.”
**Rubric /12:** accurate attribution 4, quotation/paraphrase handling 2, synthesis 2, limits 2, next evidence 2. **Exit:** explain why changing words alone does not remove the need to credit a source. **Homework:** audit every factual claim in the paragraph against its source.

## EN12-B — Extended analytical essay and structural revision
**Prerequisite:** EN12 evidence-led recommendations. **Objectives:** sustain a thesis; structure competing considerations; revise paragraph functions rather than merely correct spelling.

**Prompt:** “Should every school project be assessed through group work?” Discuss using the original case notes below. This is a reasoning exercise, not a report of real educational research.
**Case notes:** In one fictional class, a four-person design project produced a detailed model. Two members said they learned from discussion; another reported that tasks were distributed unevenly. The teacher saw the finished model but kept no records of individual contributions. In a separate individual writing task, all learners submitted work, but there was no measure comparing learning across the two activities.

**Teach:** A defensible thesis can be conditional: “Group work has value, but it should not replace every individual assessment.” Product quality and individual learning are distinct. Structure: thesis → benefits → limitations → conditions/counterargument → conclusion. A counterargument needs a fair reply, not dismissal. Model reply: “Individual tasks can also disadvantage learners who benefit from discussion; preparation may therefore be collaborative even when part of the assessed evidence is individual.”

**Guided:** label paragraph purposes in a five-paragraph outline. Flag the unsupported claim “the group learned more because its model was detailed.” Suggest evidence that would distinguish contributions without intrusive surveillance.

**Independent:** write 300–350 words, making a clear thesis, using three accurate case details, addressing one opposing argument and explaining the evidence limits. Then produce a reverse outline: write each paragraph's actual function in one sentence. Move or remove a paragraph that repeats another. End with a 40-word revision note explaining what improved.

**Teacher key:** either a mixed model or a carefully qualified alternative can succeed. Do not infer causal learning gains from different tasks or reports from a few members. Useful proposals: agreed task roles, short individual explanations and a published rubric, subject to school policy. Sample conclusion: “The case supports using group work selectively, not making it universal. Shared products can reveal collaboration, while individual explanations can help establish what each learner understands.”
**Rubric /20:** thesis/structure 4, accurate evidence 4, analysis/limits 4, counterargument 4, revision/language 4. **Exit:** name one difference between polishing a sentence and improving an argument. **Homework:** revise the essay using the reverse outline, retaining a change log.

# Computing

## CS08-B — Choosing and reading a chart
**Suggested Grade 8 · Objective:** select a chart that fits a question, label units and check whether a display gives a misleading impression. **Retrieval:** what is a category, and what is a numerical count?

**Model:** An invented school club poll lists Art 8, Science 12, Reading 10. To compare categories, use equal-width bars on a scale beginning at zero; label the count axis “responses”. Science has 4 more responses than Art. A scale beginning at 7 would visually exaggerate the difference between counts of 8 and 12. A line joining art to science suggests an ordering the categories may not have.

**Guided task:** Make the bar chart on grid paper or with a spreadsheet chart tool using only these fictional category totals. Show a data table as a text alternative. Compare the view from a zero baseline to a scale beginning at seven. Do not collect classmates’ names.

**Independent practice and key:**
1. Which chart suits comparing counts for three club choices? **A labelled bar chart.**
2. For school library visits recorded as Monday 6, Tuesday 9, Wednesday 7, which display shows direction over time? **A labelled line chart with days ordered is reasonable; dots show recorded values, not visits measured between them.**
3. Art rose from 8 to 10 responses in a second fictional poll. What extra information would help assess whether its share grew? **Total respondents in both polls, and whether respondents chose under comparable rules.**
4. Correct the title “Every pupil prefers science.” **“Science was the highest-count club choice in this fictional poll.”**

**Formative check:** require an informative title, labelled scale, selected chart justified and interpreted claim. **Exit:** name one misleading display choice and how to fix it. **Example:** cut-off bar baseline; restart at zero and label units.

## CS09-B — Dry runs and decision flowcharts
**Scope:** Punjab Grade9 annual2027 Computer Science & Entrepreneurship **model-paper-informed skills practice**, limited to the source distinction above, not full syllabus certification. **Prerequisite:** variables and comparisons. **Objectives:** trace changes manually; explain what a dry run can reveal; select and connect standard flowchart symbols.

**Teach:** A dry run follows an algorithm step by step with chosen input, without executing it on a computer. Record the state after each assignment. Testing a few cases can expose errors but cannot prove every possible input is correct. Flowchart conventions here: oval=start/end; parallelogram=input/output; rectangle=process/assignment; diamond=decision with labelled exits; arrows=direction.

**Original case A:** a classroom materials counter records exactly three nonnegative whole-number donations. It starts at0 and outputs the total. This is a simulation, not money collection.
```
total ← 0
FOR count ← 1 TO 3
    INPUT boxes
    total ← total + boxes
NEXT count
OUTPUT total
```
**Worked dry run, inputs2,0,4:**
| count | boxes | total before | total after |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
|1|2|0|2|
|2|0|2|2|
|3|4|2|6|
Output6. The variable `total` must retain earlier donations; placing `total ← 0` inside the loop would incorrectly discard them.

**Original case B:** a fictional cupboard has a capacity of20 boxes. Input a proposed whole-number count. Reject negatives; otherwise display “Space available” when count<20, and “Capacity reached or exceeded” otherwise. It reports a condition, not permission to overload physical storage.
**Text flowchart to draw:** Start(oval) → Input count(parallelogram) → count<0?(diamond). Yes → Output Invalid(parallelogram) → End. No → count<20?(diamond). Yes → Output Space available → End. No → Output Capacity reached or exceeded → End. Label both Yes/No branches; do not use a process rectangle for a decision.

**Guided:** draw the flowchart and trace counts19 and20 with coloured arrows. Record each evaluated condition. Never evaluate the second decision for an already rejected negative value.
**Independent:** (1) Dry-run caseA using3,1,5. (2) Give the wrong output if reset occurs at the start of every iteration. (3) Trace caseB for−1,0,20,21. (4) Explain the purpose of two different symbols. (5) Give one benefit and one limit of a dry run. (6) A pupil changes `<20` to `<=20`; which supplied test exposes the changed boundary?
**Key:** totals3,4,9; wrong final output5; −1 Invalid,0 Space available,20/21 Capacity reached or exceeded; diamond tests condition and selects route, parallelogram takes input/displays output; reveals state/logic errors but selected tests do not prove all behaviour;20 exposes change.
**Rubric /12:** trace4, flowchart symbols/arrows4, boundary2, explanation/limit2. **Exit:** explain the difference between tracing an algorithm and guessing its final answer. **Support:** use counters and a trace template. **Stretch:** specify a policy for non-integer input before extending the algorithm; do not silently treat text input as a number.

## CS10-B — Functions, parameters and systematic debugging
**Prerequisite:** CS10 loops and validation. **Objectives:** separate a returned value from printed output; trace parameters; use tests to locate a defect. **Resources:** paper or offline Python3.
**Teach:** A parameter names an input inside a function; an argument supplies a value at a call. Returning passes a result to the caller. Printing displays a value but does not substitute for a return. Original requirement: count how many supplied numbers are strictly greater than a threshold.
```
def count_above(values, threshold):
    count = 0
    for value in values:
        if value >= threshold:  # defect: requirement says strictly greater
            count += 1
    return count
```
**Worked:** values[2,5,5,8], threshold5. Defective output3; required output1. Change `>=` to `>`, not the expected answer.
**Guided:** record value, condition and count at each iteration. Discuss why a test with no value equal to threshold might miss the defect.
**Independent:** (1) Correct the function. (2) Predict results for([],5), ([5],5), ([6,9],5), ([-2,0],-1). (3) Explain the effect of replacing `return count` with `print(count)` when assigning `answer=count_above(...)`. (4) Suggest one invalid-input policy without implementing a network service.
**Key:** use `>`; outputs0,0,2,1; function prints but implicitly returnsNone, so answer becomesNone; reject nonnumeric values or document numeric-only preconditions. The validation policy must be explicit, not guessed.
**Rubric /10:** correction2, tests4, return explanation2, policy2. **Exit:** name a boundary test and explain its diagnostic value. **Support:** hand trace one item first. **Homework:** create a second function counting values below a threshold and provide empty/boundary/normal tests. Python here is teaching syntax, not a claim about permitted exam-answer language.

## CS11-B — Database normalisation and update anomalies
**Prerequisite:** CS11 primary/foreign keys and SELECT. **Objectives:** identify update anomalies; separate independent facts; explain parameters versus string concatenation. **No production access.**
**Synthetic table:** Booking(booking_id, learner_id, learner_alias, course_id, course_title). Rows: (B1,L1,A,C1,Writing), (B2,L1,A,C2,Algebra), (B3,L2,B,C1,Writing). Assume each learner ID has one current alias and each course ID one current title.
**Teach:** Copying course titles into bookings risks inconsistent updates. Store Learner(learner_id,alias), Course(course_id,title), Booking(booking_id,learner_id,course_id). Preserve the declared dependencies; normalisation is not just creating many tables. A parameterized query keeps SQL structure separate from values. Placeholder syntax varies by database driver.
**Worked design:** conceptual query `SELECT booking_id FROM Booking WHERE learner_id = ?`, with learner ID supplied separately through the driver's parameter interface. This is not runnable SQL for every engine. Parameters do not themselves establish who may access another learner's records; authorisation still requires separate controls.
**Guided:** change C1's title in the original design and count affected rows (two); compare one Course row in the decomposed design. Identify a booking that would fail a foreign-key check.
**Independent:** (1) List the three primary keys. (2) Which fields in Booking are foreign keys? (3) Why avoid constructing SQL by joining a text value into its statement? (4) Does using a parameter prove the requesting user owns L2? (5) Retrieve B1/B2 conceptually by learner ID and explain the join needed to show course titles.
**Key:** learner_id,course_id,booking_id in their respective tables; Booking learner_id/course_id; input should remain data rather than become query structure; no; filter Booking learner_id=L1, join Course on matching course_id for titles. Use only fictional IDs, no attack against a live endpoint.
**Rubric /12:** schema4, integrity2, parameter explanation2, authorisation distinction2, join2. **Exit:** explain one anomaly prevented and one security issue not solved by normalisation. **Homework:** draw dependency arrows and state assumptions that would change the model.

## CS12-B — Insertion sort, invariants and operation counts
**Prerequisite:** CS12 search scaling and ordered lists. **Objectives:** trace insertion sort; state a loop invariant; distinguish operation counts from elapsed time.
**Teach:** Insertion sort keeps a sorted prefix and inserts the next item into it. Before each outer pass, the prefix already processed is sorted and contains exactly its original items. Shift larger elements right, then insert the saved key. This is different from repeatedly swapping arbitrary pairs.
**Worked list [7,3,5,2]:** insert3 → [3,7,5,2]; insert5 → [3,5,7,2]; insert2 → [2,3,5,7]. Shift counts by pass:1,1,3; total5. Count only assignments that move an existing prefix item one place right, not saving/inserting the key.
**Pseudocode (zero-based):**
```
FOR i ← 1 TO length(A)-1
    key ← A[i]
    j ← i-1
    WHILE j >= 0 AND A[j] > key
        A[j+1] ← A[j]
        j ← j-1
    ENDWHILE
    A[j+1] ← key
NEXT i
```
This assumes short-circuit evaluation: do not read A[j] after j becomes−1. If the chosen language lacks that guarantee, split the boundary and value tests safely.
**Guided:** trace [4,1,3] and label the sorted prefix after each pass. Explain why the saved key must not be overwritten while shifting.
**Independent:** (1) Trace [2,2,1]. (2) Count shifts. (3) Give the number of shifts for already sorted [1,2,3,4]. (4) For reverse order length4, count shifts. (5) Explain the effect of changing `>` to `>=` on equal items' relative order. (6) Why does a lower operation count not give an exact runtime prediction?
**Key:** after first pass[2,2,1], second[1,2,2];2 shifts;0 shifts;1+2+3=6 shifts; `>=` can move a later equal key ahead of earlier equal items, losing stability; runtime also depends on implementation/hardware and other work. Best-case comparisons still grow with list length even with zero shifts; worst-case shifts grow quadratically. Neither statement is a guaranteed stopwatch result.
**Rubric /12:** trace4, counts3, stability2, boundary safety1, complexity/runtime distinction2. **Exit:** state the invariant and why it supports correctness. **Support:** labelled equal-value cards show stability. **Homework:** compare insertion sort for a nearly sorted small list with a different method only after defining which operations are counted.
