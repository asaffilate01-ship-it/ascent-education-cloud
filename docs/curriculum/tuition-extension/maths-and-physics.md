# UniPathway: Maths and Physics extension lessons, Grades 8–12

Ten original teacher-review drafts, one per subject per grade. These are suggested placements, not syllabus or examination-board mappings. All values are invented for teaching. Physics activities are paper-based, with no equipment required unless a teacher opts for a supervised demonstration.

Use a 45-minute structure for each lesson: 5 minutes retrieval, 10 minutes modelling, 10 minutes guided work, 15 minutes independent practice and feedback, and 5 minutes for the exit check. Cover the answer key while learners work. Use calculators where allowed and retain unrounded intermediate values.

## MA08-EXT — Grade 8 Maths: A mean for unequal group sizes

**Objective.** Calculate a combined mean and explain why two group means cannot usually be averaged directly. **Retrieve:** mean = total ÷ count; find the total if 4 scores average 7.

**Model.** A greenhouse class measures seedling heights. Group A has 6 seedlings averaging 12 cm. Group B has 4 averaging 17 cm. Reconstruct totals: 72 cm and 68 cm. Pool totals and counts: (72 + 68) ÷ (6 + 4) = 14 cm. Simply taking (12 + 17) ÷ 2 treats the groups as equal. The larger group pulls the combined mean closer to 12 cm. If given means are rounded, the reconstructed overall mean is only an estimate.

**Guided.** Draw two boxes for number and total at each club table: 3 learners average 8 model pieces; 5 average 12. Assign a partner to check that each learner, not each group, has equal weighting.

**Independent.**
1. Library teams of 7 and 3 learners average 6 and 10 books shelved. Find the combined mean.
2. Two equal groups average 18 and 26 marks. What is their combined mean? Why can you average their means here?
3. Eight seedlings average 15 cm. Adding one seedling gives a mean of 16 cm. How tall was the added seedling?

**Key.** Retrieval 28. Guided: (24 + 60) ÷ 8 = 10.5. Independent: (42 + 30) ÷ 10 = 7.2; 22 because counts match; 9 × 16 − 8 × 15 = 24 cm.

**Exit.** Which is closer to the pooled mean: the 20-person group's mean of 11 or the 2-person group's mean of 20? **Key:** 11; 20 observations have that group weighting.

## MA09-EXT — Grade 9 Maths: Simultaneous equations by elimination

**Objective.** Translate a two-quantity problem into two equations, eliminate a variable, and verify both prices in both conditions. **Retrieve:** evaluate 2x + 3y when x = 4, y = 2.

**Model.** Two rulers and three pencils cost Rs 210; two rulers and one pencil cost Rs 130. If r and p are unit prices, 2r + 3p = 210 and 2r + p = 130. First minus second gives 2p = 80, so p = 40. Substitute: 2r + 40 = 130, so r = 45. Check: 90 + 120 = 210 and 90 + 40 = 130. Explain why subtraction must apply to both sides and all terms.

**Guided.** Solve x + 2y = 14 and 2x + 3y = 23. Double the first equation before subtracting the second. Compare with solving one equation for x first.

**Independent.**
1. Solve 3a + 2b = 23 and a + 2b = 13.
2. Two folders and one notebook cost Rs 260; one folder and two notebooks cost Rs 310. Find both prices.
3. Compare x + y = 8 with 2x + 2y = 15. Can both be true? What if the second right-hand side were 16?

**Key.** Retrieval 14. Guided: 2x + 4y = 28, y = 5, x = 4. Independent: a = 5, b = 4; folder Rs 70, notebook Rs 120; no solution at 15 because doubling gives 16, infinitely many real pairs at 16 because both equations describe the same line.

**Exit.** For 2u + v = 11 and u + v = 7, find u and v, and check. **Key:** u = 4, v = 3; 8 + 3 = 11 and 4 + 3 = 7.

## MA10-EXT — Grade 10 Maths: Two draws without replacement

**Objective.** Build a probability tree for dependent draws and add probabilities of mutually exclusive paths. **Retrieve:** simplify 6/15.

**Model.** A bag holds 3 red and 2 blue tokens, each equally likely at every draw. Draw two without replacing the first. If red is first (3/5), blue is second with probability 2/4; if blue is first (2/5), red is second with probability 3/4. The two paths for exactly one red are RB and BR. Multiply along each path, then add: (3/5)(2/4) + (2/5)(3/4) = 3/5. Both second-stage denominators are 4; the numerators depend on what was removed.

**Guided.** For the same bag, find the probability of two reds, two blues and matching colours. Verify that matching plus different colours gives 1. If useful, enumerate ordered pairs of individually numbered tokens and compare.

**Independent.**
1. A bag contains 4 green and 2 yellow tokens. Find the probability of two greens without replacement.
2. For the same bag, find the probability of at least one yellow.
3. Return the first token before drawing the second. Recalculate both answers and explain the difference.

**Key.** Retrieval 2/5. Guided: 3/10, 1/10, 2/5. Independent: (4/6)(3/5) = 2/5; 1 − 2/5 = 3/5; two greens (4/6)² = 4/9, at least one yellow 5/9. Returning the token restores the original composition.

**Exit.** If the bag starts with 1 orange and 4 white, what is P(two orange without replacement)? **Key:** zero; the orange branch leaves none.

## MA11-EXT — Grade 11 Maths: Geometric sequences and finite sums

**Objective.** Recognise a constant ratio, calculate an indexed term, and distinguish a term from a partial sum. **Retrieve:** compare the changes in 3, 6, 12, 24.

**Model.** A study group reviews 8 flashcards on session 1, then 16, then 32. Assume ratio r = 2 and count review events, not necessarily distinct cards. The fifth term is 8 × 2⁴ = 128. To find the five-session total S, write S = 8 + 16 + 32 + 64 + 128 and 2S = 16 + 32 + 64 + 128 + 256. Subtract to get S = 256 − 8 = 248. In general, tₙ = arⁿ⁻¹ and Sₙ = a(rⁿ − 1)/(r − 1) for r ≠ 1. When r = 1, sum na.

**Guided.** A beacon simulator repeats 81, 27, 9, … signals in successive rounds. Find round 4 and the total through round 4. Discuss what a fractional predicted signal would mean later: the model would stop describing counts.

**Independent.**
1. For a = 5, r = 3, find the fourth term and the first four terms' sum.
2. Find the first four terms' sum for a = 64 and r = 1/2.
3. A pupil says term 6 has a factor r⁶. Explain the indexing error, then give term 6 for a = 2 and r = 2.

**Key.** Retrieval: ratios 2, differences 3, 6, 12. Guided: 3; 120. Independent: 135 and 200; 120; only five multiplications after term 1, so 64.

**Exit.** For a = 7, r = 1, find term 10 and sum 10. **Key:** 7 and 70; the quotient formula is undefined at r = 1.

## MA12-EXT — Grade 12 Maths: Vectors and perpendicular components

**Objective.** Compute dot products, test perpendicularity for nonzero vectors, and interpret signed scalar projections. **Retrieve:** magnitude of (3, 4) and the unit vector in its direction.

**Model.** A greenhouse robot makes displacement d = (6, 2) metres. The row points in the direction v = (3, 4). The unit direction is e = (3/5, 4/5). The scalar distance component along the row is d · e = 18/5 + 8/5 = 26/5 m. Its vector parallel component is (26/5)e = (78/25, 104/25) m. The perpendicular remainder is d minus that component = (72/25, −54/25) m. Dotting the remainder with v gives (216 − 216)/25 = 0. The scalar component is not the vector's full length.

**Guided.** For d = (−4, 3) and e = (1, 0), find both components and interpret the negative sign: it points opposite the chosen positive direction.

**Independent.**
1. Are (2, −3) and (6, 4) perpendicular? Verify.
2. Find the scalar projection of (4, 1) onto the direction (3, 4).
3. For (4, 1) projected onto (1, 1), find the parallel vector and perpendicular remainder. Show a dot-product check.

**Key.** Retrieval 5 and (3/5, 4/5). Guided: scalar −4, parallel (−4, 0), perpendicular (0, 3). Independent: yes, 12 − 12 = 0; 16/5; parallel (5/2, 5/2), remainder (3/2, −3/2), dot with (1, 1) is zero.

**Exit.** Can you find a unit direction for (0, 0)? **Key:** no; magnitude zero cannot be divided into components and does not specify a direction.

## PH08-EXT — Grade 8 Physics: Density and floating

**Objective.** Calculate density with consistent units and predict whether a solid object floats in water under simple conditions. **Retrieve:** find the volume of a box 2 cm × 3 cm × 4 cm.

**Model.** An unlabeled solid block has mass 54 g and volume 60 cm³. Its average density is 54 ÷ 60 = 0.90 g/cm³. In water, taken as 1.00 g/cm³, it is expected to float with some volume submerged. A solid 30 g part with volume 20 cm³ has density 1.5 g/cm³ and is expected to sink. State the assumptions: calm water, unsupported object, and neglect surface effects. Equal masses do not imply equal densities.

**Guided.** A fully submerged stone pushes a measuring cylinder reading from 45 mL to 70 mL. Since 1 mL = 1 cm³, volume is 25 cm³. With mass 65 g, find its density; identify why bubbles or partial immersion would distort the result.

**Independent.**
1. Two blocks both have 40 cm³ volume. Their masses are 32 g and 52 g. Calculate densities and predict behaviour in water.
2. A mystery object's density is 0.75 g/cm³ and volume is 80 cm³. Find its mass.
3. A 48 g waterproof sealed object occupies 60 cm³. Explain why its average density, not just the material of its shell, predicts floating.

**Key.** Retrieval 24 cm³. Guided: 2.6 g/cm³; bubbles add to measured displacement; incomplete immersion misses part of the volume. Independent: 0.80 floats, 1.30 sinks; 60 g; average density 0.80 including the interior volume, so floats under the assumptions.

**Exit.** A volume reading starts at 50 mL and ends at 72 mL; mass is 33 g. **Key:** 22 cm³, 1.5 g/cm³, sinks.

## PH09-EXT — Grade 9 Physics: Momentum in a sticking collision

**Objective.** Use signed velocities to calculate momentum and predict the shared velocity after two objects stick, given negligible external impulse. **Retrieve:** select a positive direction and assign signs to leftward and rightward arrows.

**Model.** Two low-friction carts approach, with right positive. Cart A is 0.50 kg at +4.0 m/s; B is 0.50 kg at −2.0 m/s. Before impact, total momentum = (0.50)(+4.0) + (0.50)(−2.0) = +1.0 kg·m/s. They stick, so total mass 1.0 kg and v = +1.0 m/s. Opposing momenta subtract; the sign means rightwards. Kinetic energy need not be conserved: sound, heating and deformation can take energy from the carts' motion.

**Guided.** A 0.30 kg cart at +2.0 m/s sticks to a stationary 0.20 kg cart. Predict the direction first, then calculate. Ask why the joined cart slows despite momentum conservation.

**Independent.**
1. 0.60 kg at +3.0 m/s sticks to 0.40 kg at −2.0 m/s. Find the final velocity.
2. For the same masses and first velocity, what initial B velocity makes the pair stop?
3. For the worked example, compare total kinetic energy before and after, using Eₖ = ½mv².

**Key.** Retrieval right +, left − under this convention. Guided +1.2 m/s; same momentum distributed over greater mass. Independent: +1.0 m/s; −4.5 m/s; 5.0 J before and 0.50 J after, difference 4.5 J transferred from kinetic energy.

**Exit.** If both carts are rightward before a sticking collision, could the final velocity be leftward with negligible external impulse? **Key:** no; total momentum starts positive.

## PH10-EXT — Grade 10 Physics: Images made by converging lenses

**Objective.** Predict image type from object position, then use the thin-lens equation in the real-image case. **Retrieve:** what distinguishes a real image from a virtual image?

**Model.** For a thin converging lens of focal length f = 10 cm, place an object at u = 30 cm (using positive distances for the real object and real image here). The lens equation is 1/f = 1/u + 1/v. Thus 1/v = 1/10 − 1/30 = 1/15; v = 15 cm on the opposite side. A ray parallel to the axis goes through the far focus; a ray through the lens centre is drawn straight. They meet, giving an inverted image. Magnification magnitude v/u = 0.50; a 4 cm object gives a 2 cm image.

**Guided.** On graph paper take f = 5 squares and u = 10 squares. Draw the two rays and compare measured image distance and height with the formula. For an object inside f, extend diverging outgoing rays backwards to locate the upright virtual image; do not treat this as a positive real image distance.

**Independent.**
1. f = 12 cm, u = 18 cm, object height = 3 cm. Find v and image height, and state orientation.
2. f = 8 cm, u = 16 cm. What are v and the magnification magnitude?
3. f = 8 cm, u = 5 cm. Describe the image and say whether a screen can catch it.

**Key.** Retrieval: real image rays physically meet, so a screen can receive it; virtual rays only appear to come from the image. Guided: v = 10 squares, same height, inverted. Independent: 36 cm and 6 cm, inverted; 16 cm and 1; virtual, upright and enlarged, no screen.

**Exit.** What happens to the outgoing rays from an on-axis point at the focal distance in this ideal model? **Key:** parallel; no finite screen position gives a focused image.

## PH11-EXT — Grade 11 Physics: Spring oscillations and period

**Objective.** Interpret displacement, restoring force and period, and test the mass–period relation for an ideal spring oscillator. **Retrieve:** frequency is cycles per second; convert a 0.5 s period to frequency.

**Model.** A mass moves on an ideal spring about equilibrium. With negligible damping and a linear spring, the net restoring force along the motion is F = −kx, where x is displacement from equilibrium. The minus sign points back toward equilibrium. The period is T = 2π√(m/k). For m = 0.25 kg and k = 16 N/m, T = 2π(0.125) ≈ 0.79 s. At x = +0.03 m the restoring force is −0.48 N. At equilibrium the restoring force is zero and speed is greatest for a moving ideal oscillator.

**Guided.** Three runs of ten cycles take 7.8, 8.0 and 7.9 s. Average the times first, divide by ten, then compare to the model. Discuss reaction time and why timing several cycles reduces its relative effect.

**Independent.**
1. With the model spring, predict T for m = 1.00 kg. Explain why quadrupling mass does not quadruple T.
2. Find the restoring force at x = −0.02 m.
3. A second spring with the same 0.25 kg mass gives a shorter period. Which spring has greater k?

**Key.** Retrieval 2 Hz. Guided 0.79 s, agreeing to two decimal places. Independent: 1.57 s, square root doubles T; +0.32 N; second spring, since T falls as k rises.

**Exit.** If k quadruples and m stays fixed, what happens to T? **Key:** halves.

## PH12-EXT — Grade 12 Physics: Wavelength from double-slit fringes

**Objective.** Use the small-angle double-slit relation with SI units, predict changes in spacing, and explain what the pattern demonstrates. **Retrieve:** convert 0.25 mm and 600 nm to metres.

**Model.** In a simulated setup with coherent monochromatic light, slit separation d = 0.30 mm and screen distance L = 1.8 m. Adjacent bright fringes have spacing s = 3.6 mm. For small angles, constructive interference occurs when path difference is a whole wavelength and s ≈ λL/d. So λ ≈ sd/L = (3.6 × 10⁻³)(3.0 × 10⁻⁴)/1.8 = 6.0 × 10⁻⁷ m = 600 nm. Bright fringes are where waves reinforce; dark fringes are where waves cancel ideally. This model is for well-resolved fringes near the centre.

**Guided.** Instead of measuring one spacing, the simulated distance from bright fringe −2 to +2 is 14.4 mm. Draw tick marks and count four gaps, not five. Compute s and explain how spanning several gaps can reduce the relative reading error.

**Independent.**
1. With λ = 500 nm, d = 0.20 mm and L = 2.0 m, find s.
2. If d doubles but λ and L stay fixed, what happens to s?
3. An experiment gives s = 2.0 mm, d = 0.40 mm and L = 1.6 m. Find λ in nm.

**Key.** Retrieval 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ m and 6.0 × 10⁻⁷ m. Guided 3.6 mm. Independent: 5.0 mm; halves; 500 nm.

**Exit.** A learner enters mm as m for s but converts d correctly. How does that affect calculated λ? **Key:** result is 1,000 times too large.
