# UniPathway Chemistry and Biology: second teaching batch

**Original draft, 20 November 2026.** Ten substantive formative lessons: Chemistry and Biology, one each for proposed Grades 8–12. These extend the written starter topics in PR #238 and develop its proposed next-lesson roadmap. Grade placement is provisional; Grade 8 topics may belong within integrated science. No board mapping, accreditation, exam coverage or equivalence is claimed. Review against the selected Pakistan board, subject group, session, curriculum and textbook edition; Cambridge routes require their own qualification, subject code and exam-year mapping.

**Delivery:** 45–55 minutes per lesson: 5-minute prerequisite check; 15-minute explanation/worked example; 10-minute guided task; 15-minute independent practice; 5-minute exit feedback. All numerical observations below are synthetic or explicitly stipulated models, not measured findings. Resources are paper, diagrams and calculators. No chemical preparation, heating, tasting, live-organism handling, genetic manipulation or bodily samples. Keep answer keys in the teacher view. Teacher feedback is formative, not an official grade. Provide large-print diagrams, spoken descriptions and vocabulary support without lowering conceptual expectations.

## CH08-B — Particle models and changes of state
**Proposed Grade 8. Prerequisite:** mixtures versus pure substances. **Objectives:** connect particle arrangement to state; distinguish changes in particle motion from changes in particle identity; interpret a simple heating curve. **Starter:** when an ice cube melts, do its individual water molecules become liquid droplets?

### Explain and model
A particle diagram represents matter at a scale too small to see directly. In a solid, particles remain near fixed positions while vibrating. Liquid particles remain close together but can move past one another. Gas particles are much farther apart on average and move throughout their available space. These descriptions simplify many-particle behaviour: the drawn circles are not literal coloured balls.

During a change of state of a pure substance, particle identity remains the same. Heating generally increases average particle kinetic energy when temperature rises. At a phase-change plateau under suitable constant-pressure conditions, transferred energy changes the arrangement and interactions rather than increasing temperature. A plateau does not mean the heater has stopped transferring energy. Real samples and equipment need not produce perfectly flat textbook lines.

**Worked example:** an idealised substance warms as a solid from −10°C to 0°C, remains at 0°C while melting, then warms as a liquid to 20°C. Label the three sections: solid warming; solid and liquid together during melting; liquid warming. The 0°C melting point is stipulated for this example, not a claim about every substance.

### Guided task
Draw six particles in each state. Annotate spacing and permitted motion. Then explain why a gas can be compressed much more readily than a liquid using the spaces between particles, not shrinking particles.

### Independent practice and key
1. Do particles cease moving in the solid model? **No: they vibrate about positions.**
2. Why can melting continue while temperature stays constant? **Energy changes the particle arrangement/interactions during the transition.**
3. Does water becoming water vapour create a new chemical substance? **No: it is a physical change of state.**
4. Correct “heated particles get bigger”. **Average motion or spacing can change; the model does not require particles themselves to expand.**

**Exit:** distinguish evaporation from boiling. **Expected:** evaporation occurs at the surface over a range of temperatures; boiling occurs throughout the liquid at the boiling temperature for the pressure. **Support:** label motion arrows before drawing a curve. **Extension:** explain why pressure must be stated when discussing a boiling point. Desk discussion only.

## CH09-B — Reading patterns in the periodic table
**Proposed Grade 9. Prerequisite:** atomic number and simplified electron shells. **Objectives:** read a period and group; make a limited prediction from a pattern; distinguish a pattern from an exact measurement. **Starter:** does moving down a group change the element's identity?

### Explain and model
Elements are ordered by atomic number, not by mass. Rows are periods and columns are groups. For early main-group elements, the period matches the number of occupied electron shells in the introductory model. Elements in the same main group often have related outer-shell arrangements and related chemistry. This is a guide, not a license to predict every property. Do not extend the simple main-group rule to transition metals.

**Worked example:** the table gives Li with atomic number 3, period 2, group 1, and Na with atomic number 11, period 3, group 1. Na has one more occupied shell than Li in the model. The two belong to the same group because both have one outer-shell electron, not because their atoms are the same size. A model with synthetic radii Li 150, Na 185 and K 225 pm suggests increasing size down these rows. Even if the trend is reasonable, a simplified dataset cannot establish a real element's exact radius; radius definition and measurement both matter.

### Guided task
Use these table entries: F (9, period 2, group 17), Cl (17, period 3, group 17), and Ne (10, period 2, group 18). Match F with a same-group element and a same-period element. Explain each choice without invoking a chemical reaction.

### Independent practice and key
1. Which comes first by atomic number, Cl (17) or F (9)? **F.**
2. Na and Cl are both in period 3. Are they in the same group? **No; they have the same number of occupied shells in this model but different outer-shell arrangements.**
3. Given an unfamiliar main-group element in group 17 directly below Cl, what is a defensible prediction? **It shares the group’s broad outer-shell pattern; its exact size or reaction rate is not established by the position alone.**
4. Correct “Every atom in one group has the same atomic number.” **Atomic numbers differ between elements; a group is a structural or property pattern, not shared identity.**

**Exit:** label a row and column, then name one prediction and one limitation. **Support:** colour each category and read values aloud. **Extension:** explain why periodic position is more useful than comparing element names.

## CH10-B — Acids, bases and interpreting indicator evidence
**Proposed Grade 10. Prerequisite:** ions, equations and ratios. **Objectives:** interpret supplied indicator results; distinguish concentration from strength; recognise what pH does and does not establish. **Starter:** can colour alone prove a liquid is safe?

### Explain and model
In the introductory aqueous model, acidic solutions have greater hydrogen-ion activity than neutral water; alkaline solutions have lower hydrogen-ion activity and greater hydroxide-ion activity. At 25°C, pH 7 is neutral. A base can accept hydrogen ions in the model used here; an alkali is a water-soluble base. These definitions should be matched to the eventual course specification.

An indicator changes colour over a limited pH range, not at every individual pH value. We therefore treat indicator colours as evidence for a range unless more precise data are supplied. Strong/weak describes the extent of ionisation for an acid in water; concentrated/dilute describes amount per solution volume. A dilute strong acid and a concentrated weak acid are possible. Neither “weak” nor a particular indicator colour guarantees safety.

**Worked example:** supplied readings at 25°C give fictional solutions P pH 3, Q pH 7 and R pH 10. Classify P acidic, Q neutral and R alkaline. An ideal universal-indicator chart may support these broad classifications, but its colour cannot establish exact concentration or identify the substance.

### Guided task
A fictional indicator is yellow below pH 6, changes through mixed colours between 6 and 8, and is blue above 8. Classify a blue result only as above pH 8. Explain why assigning exactly pH 10 goes beyond the evidence.

### Independent practice and key
1. At 25°C, classify pH 5 and 9. **Acidic and alkaline.**
2. Does equal pH imply equal chemical identity? **No.**
3. A weak acid ionises only partly. Does that tell you its concentration? **No.**
4. Can pH values be averaged to predict the pH of mixed solutions? **Not generally: reaction amounts and acid/base behaviour matter, and pH is logarithmic.**

**Exit:** write one supported and one unsupported conclusion from an indicator result. **Support:** a labelled pH line and separate strength/concentration cards. **Extension:** explain why measured data are better than guessing from a household product's label. No household testing, mixing or tasting is assigned.

## CH11-B — Enthalpy changes and energy-cycle reasoning
**Proposed Grade 11. Prerequisite:** equation coefficients, moles and signed arithmetic. **Objectives:** interpret the sign of enthalpy change; combine hypothetical reaction steps; distinguish an energy cycle from an experimental recipe. **Starter:** if a system releases energy to its surroundings, is its enthalpy change positive or negative?

### Explain and model
At constant pressure, an enthalpy change describes the heat transferred for a process under the specified conditions. An exothermic process has negative ΔH; an endothermic one has positive ΔH. The system and surroundings must be identified: a negative system change is consistent with energy reaching the surroundings.

Hess's law uses enthalpy as a state function: for identical starting and ending states, the total change does not depend on the chosen sequence of steps. Reverse a stated process and the sign reverses. Multiply its amount by a factor and multiply ΔH by that factor. States and conditions must match before combining data.

**Worked example:** hypothetical states A, B and C have A → B, ΔH = −40 kJ mol⁻¹, and B → C, ΔH = +15 kJ mol⁻¹, all for the same amount basis. Adding cancels intermediate B, giving A → C with ΔH = −25 kJ mol⁻¹. Therefore C → A is +25 kJ mol⁻¹. B is a bookkeeping intermediate; this diagram says nothing about reaction speed or whether either route is experimentally accessible.

### Guided task
Sketch A at relative energy 0, B at −40 and C at −25. Use vertical arrows to show the signs. Explain why the second step can absorb energy while the overall change releases energy.

### Independent practice and key
1. A → D is +30; D → C is −55. Overall? **−25 kJ mol⁻¹ on the same basis.**
2. Reverse D → C. **C → D, +55 kJ mol⁻¹.**
3. Energy change for 2 mol following A → C? **−50 kJ, assuming the stated molar change applies.**
4. Does negative ΔH prove a process is rapid or spontaneous? **No; rate and thermodynamic feasibility require other information.**

**Exit:** identify the error in adding steps whose intermediates have different physical states without accounting for the state change. **Support:** numerical energy ladder. **Extension:** explain why changing coefficients but leaving ΔH unchanged is inconsistent. All species and values here are abstract; no practical reaction is proposed.

## CH12-B — Weak-acid equilibrium and logarithmic pH
**Proposed Grade 12. Prerequisite:** concentration, equilibrium, logarithms and square roots. **Objectives:** relate a simple dissociation model to Ka; estimate pH with an explicit approximation; test that approximation. **Starter:** is a tenfold hydrogen-ion change a ten-unit pH change?

### Explain and model
For dilute aqueous classroom calculations, approximate pH as −log₁₀[H⁺], with the numerical concentration in mol dm⁻³ understood relative to the standard concentration. More rigorously pH uses activity; this lesson uses the dilute-solution approximation. A hypothetical monoprotic acid dissociates as HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻. With initial concentration c and dissociation x, Ka ≈ x²/(c−x), when other hydrogen-ion sources are negligible.

If x is small compared with c, then c−x ≈ c and x ≈ √(Ka c). This is a checked approximation, not a rule that always works.

**Worked example:** let Ka = 1.0×10⁻⁵ and c = 0.100 mol dm⁻³. Then x ≈ √(1.0×10⁻⁶) = 1.0×10⁻³ mol dm⁻³; pH ≈ 3.00. The estimated dissociation fraction is x/c = 0.010, or 1%, supporting the small-x approximation for introductory work. If needed, solve x² + Ka x − Ka c = 0 using the positive root.

### Guided task
For c = 0.0100 with the same Ka, estimate x = 3.16×10⁻⁴ and pH ≈ 3.50. The estimated dissociation is 3.16%. Explain why tenfold dilution did not increase pH by a whole unit in this weak-acid approximation.

### Independent practice and key
1. If [H⁺] = 1.0×10⁻⁴, pH? **4.00.**
2. How does [H⁺] compare at pH 3 and 4? **It is ten times greater at pH 3.**
3. Ka = 1.0×10⁻⁶, c = 0.100: estimate x and pH. **3.16×10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³; approximately 3.50.**
4. If estimated x is 40% of c, may c−x be replaced confidently by c? **No; use a more complete calculation.**

**Exit:** name two limitations: **activity approximations and neglected water contribution/other acid sources**, or the small-x assumption. **Support:** powers-of-ten log table. **Extension:** discuss why very dilute solutions require accounting for water. No solutions are to be prepared.

## BI08-B — Food webs and energy-transfer diagrams
**Proposed Grade 8. Prerequisite:** cells and organisms. **Objectives:** read feeding arrows consistently; distinguish energy flow from matter cycling; predict qualified consequences of a food-web change. **Starter:** does a plant obtain its food energy by eating soil?

### Explain and model
A producer can build organic substances using an energy source; green plants typically use light. Consumers obtain organic matter by feeding. In our food-web convention, an arrow points from food to consumer, indicating the direction of energy transfer. For grass → grasshopper → frog, the frog eats the grasshopper, not the reverse. State the convention because an unlabelled arrow can be ambiguous.

Energy is transferred between organisms, but not all energy taken in becomes new biomass available to the next consumer. Respiration transfers energy to support life processes, with energy ultimately dispersed as heat; some material is uneaten or leaves as waste. Decomposers use organic material from different levels. Matter can cycle through an ecosystem, while energy requires continuing input and is not recycled in the same way.

**Worked example:** a fictional yearly diagram assigns 8,000 kJ to plant biomass production and 800 kJ to herbivore biomass production over the same area and period. Transfer efficiency on this stated basis is 800/8,000 × 100 = 10%. This example is not a universal 10% law and does not imply that energy was destroyed.

### Guided task
Draw grass → grasshopper → frog and grass → rabbit → fox. Add dead organic material → decomposers from more than one level. Explain why a fall in grass availability could affect both pathways.

### Independent practice and key
1. In seed → bird, what does the arrow mean? **Transfer from eaten seed to bird.**
2. Transfer from 600 kJ to 90 kJ: efficiency? **15%.**
3. Must fewer frogs cause more grasshoppers? **It may, but other predators, food supply and conditions can alter the outcome.**
4. Are decomposers only connected to top predators? **No: material reaches them from many levels.**

**Exit:** explain why removing a species may have indirect effects. **Support:** organism cards with labelled arrows. **Extension:** add an alternative grasshopper predator and revise the prediction. No animal collection or feeding experiment is required.

## BI09-B — Photosynthesis and limiting-factor evidence
**Proposed Grade 9. Prerequisite:** diffusion, plant cells and graph reading. **Objectives:** describe the inputs and outputs of photosynthesis; interpret a plateau; distinguish a proxy measurement from total photosynthetic activity. **Starter:** does doubling light always double plant growth?

### Explain and model
Photosynthesis uses light energy to support the formation of organic substances from carbon dioxide and water, releasing oxygen in the familiar simplified overall description. Chlorophyll helps capture light. Plants also respire, including when illuminated. A measured net oxygen output therefore need not equal the gross oxygen production from photosynthesis.

A limiting factor restricts a process under particular conditions. Increasing one input may initially increase rate, then cease to help because another factor limits it. A plateau alone identifies a pattern, not automatically the exact limiting factor. Temperature, carbon dioxide supply, plant condition and measurement constraints can all matter.

**Worked example:** fictional readings pair light index 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 with net oxygen-output index 2, 4, 6, 6, 6. Between light indices 1 and 3, output rises; after 3 it plateaus. It is reasonable to propose another limitation, but not to claim carbon dioxide shortage is proven. A comparison changing carbon dioxide supply while controlling other conditions could test that explanation using supplied datasets.

### Guided task
A second synthetic dataset under increased carbon dioxide supply gives outputs 2, 4, 7, 9, 9 at the same light indices. Explain why this supports carbon dioxide limitation in part of the first dataset without proving that it limits every point.

### Independent practice and key
1. Change in output from light 1 to 3 in the first set? **+4 index units.**
2. Percentage increase from 2 to 6? **200%.**
3. Why hold temperature constant in the comparison? **It can also influence rate.**
4. Does the plateau mean photosynthesis stops? **No: output remains positive and approximately constant in the data.**

**Exit:** separate observation, explanation and further evidence in three sentences. **Support:** plot one point together and label both axes. **Extension:** discuss why plant growth depends on more than a short-term rate measure. Use provided data only; no live biological investigation is assigned.

## BI10-B — Homeostasis as negative feedback
**Proposed Grade 10. Prerequisite:** biological organisation and simple cause/effect diagrams. **Objectives:** identify sensor, coordination and response; explain negative feedback; distinguish a stable range from an unchanging value. **Starter:** does “negative” mean that feedback is harmful?

### Explain and model
Homeostasis maintains internal conditions within ranges compatible with function despite disturbance. In a negative-feedback model, a departure from a reference condition triggers responses that oppose that departure. A sensor detects a condition, coordination links information to action, and effectors produce responses. As the deviation becomes smaller, the correcting response can reduce. “Negative” refers to opposing change, not to whether a response is good or bad.

A thermostat analogy illustrates the structure: falling room temperature can trigger heating; rising temperature towards the reference reduces heating. Living systems are more complex, with multiple interacting signals and varying reference conditions. Body temperature is not identical everywhere or fixed at precisely one value throughout the day. This lesson is not guidance for assessing illness or treating temperature changes.

**Worked example:** in an abstract model, reference value = 10 units. A disturbance lowers the measured value to 8. A response adds 1 unit per step until the value reaches 10, then switches off. Values are 8 → 9 → 10. A response that instead removed another unit would amplify the deviation, not provide negative feedback for this disturbance.

### Guided task
Draw disturbance → detected change → coordination → opposing response → reduced deviation. Add a feedback arrow from the resulting condition to detection. Explain why the response should not continue at full strength indefinitely in this model.

### Independent practice and key
1. Is a constant response necessarily negative feedback? **No: its effect must oppose the deviation and be regulated by information about the condition.**
2. For a high value of 12, what action moves towards 10? **A reducing response.**
3. Does maintaining a range mean every reading is identical? **No.**
4. Why is a thermostat an analogy, not a complete account of human regulation? **Biological systems involve interacting processes and variable conditions beyond one switch.**

**Exit:** explain the difference between disturbance and response. **Support:** use coloured arrows for increase/decrease. **Extension:** discuss how a delayed response could cause overshoot in a model. Do not collect learners' physiological readings or medical histories.

## BI11-B — Comparing respiratory-rate data
**Proposed Grade 11. Prerequisite:** ratios and biological energy transfer. **Objectives:** distinguish breathing from cellular respiration; calculate per-minute rates from equal or unequal windows; judge a biological comparison. **Starter:** can an increased breathing rate directly measure ATP production?

### Explain and model
Ventilation moves air into and out of the respiratory system. Gas exchange transfers oxygen and carbon dioxide across a surface. Cellular respiration is a set of cellular processes that transfers energy from food molecules; aerobic respiration uses oxygen. Breathing rate alone does not measure the amount of air per breath or the rate of cellular respiration. In this desk lesson no learner is asked to exercise or provide a personal measurement.

**Worked example:** in an entirely fictional observation, model X has 8 breaths counted over 40 seconds and model Y has 9 counted over 30 seconds. To compare rates use counts × 60 ÷ seconds: X = 12 breaths/min, Y = 18 breaths/min. This supports a difference in frequency in the two windows but cannot reveal volume per breath, oxygen uptake or disease. Counts near a boundary or short measurement windows can produce uncertainty.

### Guided task
Compare fictional model A (10 breaths in 50 s) and B (12 in 40 s). Work in the same units before comparing. **Key:** 12 and 18 breaths/min, respectively. Add a note stating what frequency does not measure.

### Independent practice and key
1. Convert 7 breaths in 35 s to breaths/min. **12.**
2. A counts 18 breaths in 60 s; B counts 12 in 40 s. Which frequency is greater? **Neither; both are 18/min.**
3. Name one reason isolated short counts might differ. **Natural variation and/or counting uncertainty; comparisons should use consistent conditions and repeated windows.**
4. Evaluate “A difference of 6 breaths/min shows a specific amount of extra oxygen entered the blood.” **Unsupported; breathing pattern, volume and gas exchange are not directly measured.**

**Exit:** explain ventilation and respiration in your own words; name one additional variable needed to estimate total air moved per minute. **Key:** ventilation moves air; cellular respiration transfers energy through cellular reactions; volume per breath is needed. No clinical interpretation or personal test.

## BI12-B — Gene expression: from a model to evidence
**Proposed Grade 12. Prerequisite:** genes, proteins, inheritance and enzyme function. **Objectives:** distinguish transcription from translation at a conceptual level; explain why similar DNA can support different cell functions; evaluate gene-expression evidence without overclaiming. **Starter:** do all cells containing a gene necessarily make the same amount of its product?

### Explain and model
For a protein-coding gene in a simplified eukaryotic model, transcription produces an RNA copy using DNA information. RNA processing precedes translation, in which ribosomes use messenger RNA information to assemble an amino-acid chain. The resulting protein's folding and subsequent processing affect its function. Some genes produce functional RNAs rather than proteins, so “every gene makes a protein” is too broad.

Different cell types can use different subsets of largely shared genetic information. Regulation at several stages means RNA abundance and protein abundance need not change in the same proportion. A measured association is not proof that a single gene controls a complex trait or that changing it would produce a predictable outcome. This lesson uses abstract labels only, with no sequence design or experimental manipulation.

**Worked example:** synthetic cells A and B have the same hypothetical gene G. Relative G messenger-RNA readings are A = 10 and B = 30 units; relative protein readings are A = 20 and B = 30 units on a separate scale. RNA is threefold higher in B, while protein is 1.5-fold higher. Ratios within each measurement type are meaningful here; dividing the RNA reading by the protein reading does not establish a molecule count or conversion efficiency.

### Guided task
Construct DNA information → RNA → protein, then annotate potential regulation at transcription, RNA persistence, translation and protein persistence. Explain why the diagram describes information use rather than DNA being consumed to become protein.

### Independent practice and key
1. Which stage produces RNA from DNA information? **Transcription.**
2. Which uses messenger RNA to assemble an amino-acid chain? **Translation.**
3. Percentage increase in the example's protein reading? **(30−20)/20 × 100 = 50%.**
4. Does threefold RNA guarantee threefold functional protein? **No: subsequent regulation, persistence and processing can differ.**
5. Can these two cell readings prove what caused the difference? **No: replication, controls and alternative explanations matter.**

**Exit:** explain one limitation of the simple DNA → RNA → protein diagram. **Support:** stage/function matching cards. **Extension:** suggest additional types of observations needed to assess reliability, without designing a genetic experiment. Do not use personal genetic data, pathogens, biological engineering or medical interpretations.
